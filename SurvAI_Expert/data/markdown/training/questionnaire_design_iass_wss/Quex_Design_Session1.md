The goal of questionnaire design is to reduce measurement error

### Measure the wrong concept (specification error)

**Last week, did you work full-time, part-time, or did you not work at all?**

**Yes, full-time**

**Yes, part-time**

**No**

### Measure the right concept imperfectly (operationalization error)

**Last week, did you work full-time, part-time, or did you not work at all?**

**Yes, full-time**

**Yes, part-time**

**No**

### Measure the right concept “perfectly”, but R’s response <> R’s true value (response error)

**Last week, did you work full-time, part-time, or did you not work at all?**

**Yes, full-time**

**Yes, part-time**

**No (->What is the reason you didn’t work at all last week?)**

**General guidelines on writing survey questions**

1 Use simple, familiar words (avoid technical terms, jargon, and slang)

2 Use simple syntax

3 Avoid words with ambiguous meanings (i.e., aim for wording that all respondents will interpret in the same way)

4 Strive for wording that is specific and concrete (as opposed to general and abstract)

5 Make response options exhaustive and mutually exclusive

6 Avoid leading or loaded questions that push respondents toward an answer

7 Ask about one thing at a time (avoid double-barreled questions)

8 Avoid questions with single or double negations

# What makes a survey question sensitive?

* **Intrusive or out of bounds for ordinary conversations**
  * e.g., Sexual behaviors and conceptive use (NSFG)
  * Risks offending all

* **Threats or risks of disclosing certain information (to survey organization or third party)**
  * e.g., citizenship status; social security number

* **Invoking social desirability concerns**
  * Socially desirable behaviors/attitudes (e.g., voting) and socially undesirable behaviors/attitudes (e.g., driving under influence of alcohol)
  * Sensitive only to those with undesirable behaviors/attitudes

Tourangeau and Yan (2007); Yan (2021)

* **Some sensitive questions are sensitive for more than one reason**
  * e.g., criminal justice involvement; drug use

* **Sensitivity is not a stable characteristics of survey questions and varies by**
  * where and how questions are asked
  * who answers it
  * Culture
  * Time

# Optimizing for Mobile Web Surveys

* Use a scrolling design with eight to nine questions per page
* Use larger controls, larger controls enclosed in wide buttons, or wide buttons without any control to improve respondents' tapping accuracy
* Avoid grids and use item-by-item format
* Avoid horizontal alignment of response options
* Avoid features such as visual analog scales and date-picker wheels
* Use a modular design to break a long survey into a series of shorter surveys

# Multimode Survey Considerations

* Key goal is to reduce mode effects, an (unknown) combination of
  * Mode selection effect (different population subgroups responding to different modes)
  * Mode measurement effect (people giving different answers to different modes)
* Two design approaches
  * Unimode approach aims to reduce mode effects by using equivalent stimuli across modes, maintaining measurement equivalence across modes
    * Used when analytic goal is to make comparisons or when questions are subjective in nature (Tourangeau, 2017)
  * Best practices approach aims to minimize error within each mode separately by using each mode to its full potential, minimizing overall systematic measurement error
    * Used when analytical goal is to make overall estimates or when questions are factual (Tourangeau, 2017)

# Multimode Survey Considerations

1. Avoid mode-specific question structures when possible and use the same question format and wording across modes.
2. Use similar visual format across modes.
3. Use similar wording and visual formats across web and telephone surveys.
4. When mixing web and mail, leverage web technologies when they will help respondents navigate the questionnaire or reduce errors, but not when they will likely result in measurement differences.
5. When mixing web or mail with telephone, build in conversational cues and transition statements to unify the design across modes.
6. When mixing web or paper with telephone, give priority in both modes to the short and simple stimuli needed for telephone.
7. If there is even a small chance of mixing modes, design the questionnaire for the possibility of mixed-mode data collection.
8. Recognize that even with unified mode design, some measurement differences may still occur across modes.
