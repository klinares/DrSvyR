**Conrad, F.G., Couper, M.P., & Saksaugh, J. W. (2016). Classifying open-ended reports: Coding occupation in the current population survey.**

The study examined the reliability of occupation coding in surveys, focusing on the impact of description length and the use of coding rules.

Key Findings:

* Longer descriptions lead to lower agreement: Longer descriptions, especially those with multiple occupations and duties, tend to have lower agreement rates among coders.
* Word difficulty and length interaction: For easy words, longer descriptions decrease agreement, while for difficult words, longer descriptions slightly increase agreement.
* Coders rely on arbitrary rules: Coders often use informal rules based on superficial features of descriptions, which may improve reliability but not necessarily validity.

Implications:

* Improve interviewer training: Train interviewers to elicit concise and relevant descriptions.
* Enhance coder training: Provide coders with a set of challenging cases to discuss and reach consensus on.
* Formalize and evaluate coding rules: Make explicit and document coding rules, discarding those that don't improve validity.
* Improve coding software: Enhance software usability and incorporate decision criteria to support coders.
* Consider alternative data collection modes: Explore the impact of self-administered questionnaires on description length and agreement.

Limitations:

* Lack of individual coder information: The study did not account for individual coder experience and competence.
* Focus on interviewer-administered surveys: The findings may not generalize to self-administered surveys.

Overall, the study highlights the importance of careful consideration of description length and the use of well-founded coding rules to improve the accuracy and reliability of occupation coding in surveys.

**Malhotra, N. & Krosnick, J. (2007). Procedures for updating classification systems: A study of biotechnology and the standard occupational classification.**

The study proposes a method for identifying and addressing omissions in classification systems, such as the Standard Occupational Classification (SOC), using cognitive psychology techniques. The method involves three main steps:

1. Implicit Compatibility Assessment:

* Participants sort job descriptions into piles based on perceived similarity.
* Consensus pile labels are identified, and potential SOC additions are proposed based on these labels.

2. Explicit Identification of New and Emerging Occupations:

* Participants identify new and emerging jobs from a list of descriptions.
* Consensus pile labels are assigned to these jobs, and potential SOC additions are proposed.
* Used timing, confidence, and fit to create a category.

3. Cluster Analysis of Optimal Job Structure:

* Cluster analysis is used to group similar job descriptions.
* Consensus pile labels and SOC codes are assigned to each cluster.
* Disparities between cluster labels and SOC codes identify potential omissions in the SOC.

Key Findings:

* The three methods identified four potential additions to the SOC for biotechnology jobs:
* Clinical Trials Managers
* Manufacturing Process Designers and Technicians
* Quality Control Managers
* Business Development Managers and Analysts
* The clustering analysis emerged as the most effective method, identifying all four potential additions and no additional unnecessary ones.

Recommendations for Researchers:

* Multiple Methods: Use multiple methods for validation.
* Domain Knowledge: Require some domain knowledge to interpret results.
* Practical Considerations: Consider the practical limitations of different methods, such as time and resources.

Overall, the study demonstrates a practical and effective approach to identifying and addressing omissions in classification systems, which can be applied to various fields and industries.

**Lind, Schober, Conrad, Richert 2013 Why do survey respondents disclose more when comptuers ask the questions.**

This study investigates why people disclose less sensitive information to computers compared to human interviewers.

Key findings:

* Adding a still face to a computer interface doesn't affect disclosure of sensitive information.
* Adding a moving, speaking face with a voice can reduce disclosure compared to text-based interfaces.
* The specific characteristics of the face (realism, reaction) might not be as important as its presence.
* This suggests that the lack of a face in Audio-CASI (computer-assisted self-interviewing) might be the key factor in increased disclosure.

Future possibilities:
* Designing interview systems that tailor features (like adding faces) based on:
    * Respondent's general openness to disclosing information.
    * Whether the topic is sensitive for the respondent.
    * Respondent's emotional response to the interface.
* This personalization could improve data collection by:
    * Increasing engagement and completion rates for some respondents.
* However, there could be drawbacks like:
    * Reduced disclosure for others who find the features off-putting.

Overall, the study moves us away from the idea that computers simply lack "humanness" and suggests specific features, particularly a face, can influence how much people disclose.

**Kreuter, Presser, Tourangeau 2008**

This study compares three modes of data collection (CATI, IVR, and Web) in terms of their effectiveness in eliciting sensitive information.

Key Findings:

* Reporting of Undesirable Behaviors: Web surveys led to the highest reporting of socially undesirable behaviors (e.g., low GPA, failing grades), followed by IVR and then CATI.
* Reporting of Desirable Behaviors: There were no significant differences across modes for reporting socially desirable behaviors (e.g., high GPA, donations).
* Item Nonresponse: Web surveys had the lowest item nonresponse rates, while CATI had the highest.
* Accuracy of Reporting: Web surveys were the most accurate, followed by IVR and then CATI. This was especially true for socially undesirable behaviors.
* Perceived Question Sensitivity: Respondents in CATI perceived questions as more sensitive than those in IVR or Web.

Implications:
* Web surveys can be effective in eliciting sensitive information, particularly for socially undesirable behaviors.
* The choice of mode should consider the specific research goals and the trade-offs between response rates, item nonresponse, and accuracy.
* Future research should explore the reasons behind these mode differences and how to optimize survey designs to maximize data quality.

**Conrad, F. G., Couper, M. P., Tourangeau, R., & Zhang, C. (2017). Reducing speeding in web surveys by providing immediate feedback.**

This study investigated the impact of prompting respondents for speeding on response quality in web surveys. The authors hypothesized that prompting would increase response accuracy, reduce straightlining, and be effective across multiple waves of a longitudinal survey. Additionally, they explored the effect of asking respondents to commit to careful responding.

Methodology and Design:

*   Experiments: The study conducted six experiments with diverse samples, including volunteer web panels and a probability sample.
*   Speeding Threshold: Respondents were considered to be speeding if they answered a question in less than 350 milliseconds per word.
*   Prompting: When respondents sped, they were prompted to slow down and think more carefully about their answer.
*   Commitment: In one experiment, half of the respondents were asked to commit to answering thoughtfully and carefully.
*   Response Accuracy: Assessed by comparing correct answers to numeracy questions.
*   Straightlining: Measured by the consistency of responses to grid questions.
*   Breakoffs: Monitored to assess the impact of prompting on respondent attrition.

Key Findings:

*   Prompting Effectiveness: Prompting significantly reduced speeding across all experiments.
*   Accuracy: Prompting improved accuracy for respondents with intermediate levels of education on numeracy questions.
*   Straightlining: Prompting reduced straightlining in some experiments, suggesting a link between speeding and satisficing behavior.
*   Longitudinal Impact: Prompting remained effective across multiple waves of a longitudinal survey.
*   Commitment: Asking respondents to commit to careful responding also reduced speeding and improved accuracy for highly educated respondents.
*   Breakoffs: Prompting did not significantly increase breakoffs, suggesting minimal negative impact on respondent participation.

Overall, the study demonstrates that prompting respondents for speeding is an effective technique to improve response quality in web surveys. It can increase accuracy, reduce straightlining, and maintain effectiveness over time. Combining prompting with a commitment statement may further enhance the positive impact on response quality.

**Krosnick, J. 1991. Response strategies for coping with the cognitive demands of attitude measures in surveys.**

In his 1991 paper titled "Response strategies for coping with the cognitive demands of attitude measures in surveys," Jon Krosnick explores the idea that when faced with complex survey questions requiring significant cognitive effort, respondents often resort to "satisficing" - providing a seemingly reasonable answer rather than fully processing and integrating information, potentially leading to inaccurate or biased responses.

Key points from the paper:

Satisficing behavior:
When answering survey questions, respondents may not always engage in deep cognitive processing and instead opt for the first answer that appears satisfactory, even if it's not entirely accurate.

Factors encouraging satisficing:
* Question complexity: Complicated questions with many response options or abstract concepts can lead to satisficing.
* Time pressure: Feeling rushed during a survey can encourage respondents to quickly choose an answer without careful consideration.
* Low personal relevance: If the topic of the question is not important to the respondent, they might not invest significant cognitive effort.
* Lack of knowledge: When respondents feel they don't have enough information to answer accurately, they may resort to satisficing

**Schober, M.F., Conrad, F.G., Antoun, C., Ehlen, P., Fail, S., Hupp, A., Johnston, M., Vickers, L., Yan, H.Y., Zhang, C. (2015). Precision and disclosure in text and voice interviews on smartphones.**

This study investigated the impact of text and voice interviewing modes, as well as human and automated interviewers, on data quality in smartphone surveys. The key findings are:

Data Quality:
* Precision: Text interviews led to more precise responses, with less rounding and more differentiation in answers.
* Disclosure: Respondents were more likely to disclose socially undesirable behaviors in text interviews, especially when interacting with automated interviewers.

Interview Dynamics:
* Duration and Rhythm: Text interviews were longer but less "dense," with fewer conversational turns.
* Multitasking: Respondents were more likely to multitask during text interviews without compromising data quality.
* Respondent Experience: Respondents were generally satisfied with both text and voice interviews, with a preference for text in future surveys.

Key Methodological Points:
* Random Assignment: Participants were randomly assigned to different interview modes to control for potential biases.
* Diverse Sample: The study included a diverse sample of smartphone users.
* Data Quality Measures: Multiple measures were used to assess data quality, including rounding, straightlining, and disclosure of sensitive information.
* Multitasking Assessment: Respondents were asked about their multitasking behavior during the interviews.

Implications:
* Texting as a Promising Mode: Text interviews offer potential advantages in terms of precision and disclosure.
* Automation Benefits: Automated interviews can increase disclosure, even on mobile devices.
* Multitasking and Data Quality: Multitasking during text interviews does not necessarily harm data quality.
* Future Research: Further research is needed to explore the generalizability of these findings to different populations and interview contexts.

Overall, the study provides valuable insights into the potential of text and voice interviewing modes on smartphones for collecting high-quality survey data. It highlights the importance of considering the impact of different modes on respondent behavior and data quality.

**Schwarz, N., Oyserman, D., & Peytcheva, E. (2010). Cognition, communication, and culture: Implications for the survey response process.**

This book chapter delves into the intricate relationship between culture, cognition, and survey response. It highlights how cultural differences can significantly influence how individuals perceive, remember, and communicate information, ultimately impacting their survey responses.

Key Points:

* Cultural Differences in Memory:
    * Individualistic cultures (like the US) tend to focus on personal experiences and internal states when recalling memories.
    * Collectivist cultures (like China) often prioritize social context and relationships in their recollections.
* Language and Memory:
    * The language of a survey can influence the type of memories recalled. Using a language associated with a specific cultural context can prime certain memories and biases.
* Public vs. Private Behaviors:
    * Collectivist cultures may have a better memory for public behaviors due to the emphasis on social harmony and fitting in.
    * Individualistic cultures may rely more on estimation strategies for both public and private behaviors.
* Attitude Formation and Question Order:
    * Cultural norms and values can influence how individuals respond to attitude questions.
    * Question order effects may vary across cultures due to differences in categorization and information processing.
* Response Formatting and Editing:
    * Individualistic cultures tend to self-enhance and present themselves in a positive light.
    * Collectivist cultures may prioritize social harmony and avoid extreme responses.

Implications for Survey Research:

* Language and Context: Consider the cultural implications of the language used in surveys and the context in which they are administered.
* Question Order and Framing: Be mindful of how question order and framing can influence responses, especially across cultures.
* Response Formats: Choose response formats that are appropriate for the cultural context and the type of information being collected.
* Translation and Adaptation: Carefully translate and adapt surveys to ensure cultural equivalence and avoid unintended biases.

By understanding these cultural nuances, researchers can design surveys that are more accurate, reliable, and culturally sensitive.
