# Human versus Computer Mediation

* In interviewer-administered questionnaires, interviewer (I) mediates between computer (C) and respondent (R): I enters info on behalf of R
  - Human-Human-Computer interaction
  - Similar to interactions between doctor and patient, airline reservationist and client, etc.

$$\boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{R}\phantom{\quad}} \quad \leftrightarrows \quad \boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{I}\phantom{\quad}} \quad \leftrightarrows \quad \boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{C}\phantom{\quad}}$$

* In social media, help chats, email, etc., the computer mediates between users (Computer Mediated Communication or “CMC”)

$$\boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{U}\phantom{\quad}} \quad \leftrightarrows \quad \boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{C}\phantom{\quad}} \quad \leftrightarrows \quad \boxed{\phantom{\quad}\text{U}\phantom{\quad}}$$

R = respondent, I = interviewer, U = user, C = computer

# Intermediation

Human-human-computer interaction

- Interviewer (*Iwer*) is *surrogate user* or *intermediary*
- Respondent (*R*) is *end-user*
  * *R* interacts with computer (*C*) through *Iwer* who enters responses
- *Iwer* must split attention between *R* and *C*
  * If *R* wants to change answer, *Iwer* must back up then jump forward
  * If *R* provides answer in format not accepted by *C*, *Iwer* must translate, e.g., re-format a date or convert units
  * If *Iwer* has trouble using *C*, i.e., a *usability problem*, more attention must be devoted to *C* and less to *R*
* In general, if *Iwer* is paying attention to C, they are not attending to *R* which makes it hard to monitor *R*'s comprehension or ability to answer the question

# Intermediation (2)

* Directory Assistance operators as intermediaries (Lawrence, Atwood, Dews, 1994)
  * Like *Iwer*, Operators mediate between context of everyday life and technical discourse, e.g., translate requests into search strings likely to succeed:
    * omit words (e.g., “diner”)
    * expand customer’s abbreviations (“assoc” for “AHRC”)
    * abbreviate customer request (“C-E-N-T  H-A-R-D” for “central hardware”)
    * enter as stated “KMART” for “K-Mart”
  * Fast operators are better at typing search string while customer speaking

# Computerized Self Administration

* Human mediation (e.g., *Iwer* asks questions and records answers) is likely to introduce “social presence” – sense that someone else is there, even if on phone
* Removing *Iwer* seems to eliminate social presence and increase privacy
  – Should reduce *Rs*’ self-presentation concerns and increase disclosure of sensitive info
* Self-administered (paper) questionnaires long used to promote disclosure of sensitive information in interviews
* Computer Assisted Self Interviewing (CASI) extends idea to computerized administration
  – *Iwer* gives laptop/tablet to *R* to self-administer sensitive *Qs*
  – Textual *Qs* displayed on laptop/tablet and *R* directly enters answers
* Audio (ACASI) presentation of *Qs* assumed to further increase privacy
* More socially undesirable responses in self-administered than *Iwer*-administered mode considered evidence of greater candor
  – No incentive to falsely provide undesirable answers but external validation is rare

# CASI, ACASI and Disclosure

* Tourangeau & Smith (1996) compare data collection with and without interviewer, with and without computer
* In general, men reported 2-4 times as many heterosexual sex partners as women; presumed due to socially desirable reporting in opposite directions for men and women
* Self-administration reduced (apparent) socially desirable reporting
  – in “Women’s Health Study,” CASI led to more reported partners in past year and past 5 years than did FTF or SAQ (paper self-admin)
  – In “ACASI study,” asymmetry between sexes for # reported heterosexual sex partners is smaller for self-administration (CASI and ACASI) than for In-Person interviews (CAPI)

# Why do *Rs* disclose more in ACASI than FTF?

* Absence of human interviewer? Absence of human-like features such as a face?
* Can test by comparing disclosure in ACASI and In-Person (extremes) to disclosure with Virtual Interviewers (*VIs*), somewhere in between the extremes
  * clearly automated but embodied
* Lind, Schober, Conrad & Reichert (2013) compared prevalence of undesirable answers in 4 modes:
  * (1) FTF (In-person), (2) High Animation (HA) VI, (3) Low Animation (LA) VI, (4) ACASI
  * T&S (‘96) findings would suggest automated modes (ACASI, HA and LA) will promote disclosure relative to FTF interviews
  * But if virtual interviewers create *social presence* could inhibit disclosure, producing results more like FTF

# Lind et al. (2013) Main Findings

* Most common pattern: less disclosure in modes w/ than w/o <u>a face</u>
  – *Rs* disclose less in 8 of 10 *Qs* in FTF and both *VIs* (HA and LA) than ACASI suggesting *VIs* create social presence
  – *Rs* disclose less in FTF than the three automated modes (HA, LA, ACASI) for only 4 of 10 questions (contradicts implicit prediction by Tourangeau & Smith)
* *Rs’* comfort affected impact of mode on disclosure

# Audio vs. Text: Does ACASI feel more private than CASI?

* Immersive audio (e.g., headphones) seems to disconnect listeners from surroundings
  * Cell phones and driving (Strayer & Johnston, 2001): cell phone conversations while driving led to 2-fold increase in failure to detect traffic signs and, when signals detected, slower RTs

* But Couper, Tourangeau & Marvin (2009) find no differences between ACASI and CASI (text-only) in reporting sensitive items in survey of fertility
  * May be due to non-use of audio and incomplete playing of audio in this study
  * No difference due to presence of others
  * No literacy advantage, at least those with lowest levels of education do not report differently in ACASI vs. text-CASI
  * So not clear that ACASI actually increases *Rs*’ sense of privacy

# Does ACASI increase honesty of answers or strategy used to answer sex partners question?

* Bogus pipeline study (T & S, 1998) suggests mode effects for sensitive Qs due to more and less honest reporting
  * *R*s led to believe answers verified through polygraph-like machine
  * More sensitive behaviors reported in answering 15 of 19 Qs than in control
* But Brown and Sinclair (1999) suggest gender differences in reported number of sex partners due to gender-specific response strategies not social desirability
  * Males use “rough approximations” (general impressions) which lead to overestimation
    * Individual episodes seem hard for them to distinguish
  * Females enumerate partners which leads to underestimation
    * Individual episodes are likely distinctive and memorable

# Social Presence in Self administration?

* Does online self-administration (i.e., Web questionnaire) confer same sense of privacy as CASI and ACASI?
  - *R*s could perceive disclosure on web to be very public and harmful in contrast to laptop in interview (i.e., CASI)
* Kreuter, Presser & Tourangeau (2009) found more honest reports (compared to records) in Web than IVR or CATI
  - asked student sample sensitive Qs, e.g., “At least one D or F?”
  - # “yes”: Web > IVR > CATI
  - “False negatives” based on records: Web < IVR < CATI

# Paradox of computerization

* CASI advantage presumably because computer alone lacks the social characteristics of a human intermediary
  - CASI and ACASI lack facial representation (Lind et al., 2013)
* but “Computers are social actors” (CASA) paradigm suggests that people ascribe human attributes to computers
  - e.g., Nass & Moon, 2000 report that people are (1) Polite in evaluating the computer’s performance, and (2) Ascribe gender stereotypes to computers based on voice
* How can this be reconciled?

* Varied ease of getting definition
  – One click
  – Two clicks
  – Click and scroll
* Varied awareness of need for definition

# Ease of obtaining definitions (2)

* Generally, few respondents make use of definitions
  * Only 17.4% of respondents accessed any definitions
* Use of definitions much higher for technical terms
  * 89% of definitions accessed for technical terms, 11% for non-technical
  * Presumably, technical terms increased awareness that definitions might be needed
* The fewer clicks required to get definition, the more *Rs* requested them
  * More *Rs* accessed (any) definitions when only one click required (56%) than two clicks (24%) or click plus scroll (20%)
  * *Rs* accessed more definitions when one click required (2.69) than two clicks (1.59) or click and scroll (1.47)

# System initiative and user modeling

* Can reduce effort/initiative required to obtain clarification by automatically presenting it, *if R seems uncertain*
* Conrad, Schober & Coiner (2007), exp. 2, operationalize *R*’s uncertainty as being *inactive* for longer than required to accurately answer *Q* on basis of a straightforward scenario
* Used a single inactivity threshold for all *R*s in the “generic” treatment and different thresholds for faster (young) and slower (older) *R*s in the “group-based” treatment
  * Shorter threshold for faster than slower group
* Prediction: clarification will help more when tailored for groups of *R*s

# Progress Indicators

* Couper, Traugott & Lamias (2000) proposed that progress indicators increase # completions, i.e., reduce abandonment
  - No significant difference in completion rates with or without progress indicators
  - Additional download time of graphic may have washed out advantage
  - Perhaps indication that *R* has a long way to go is not an incentive to complete

# Summary and Conclusions

* *Iwers* seem to split their attention between computer and *R*
  - may reduce *Iwer's* awareness of *R's* experience
* Computerized <u>self-administration</u> seems to increase sense of privacy and thus honesty of answering sensitive questions
  - More undesirable answers do, in fact, reflect greater candor (compared to administrative records)
  - Increased disclosure seems to be more about absence of embodiment, especially a face, than automation/self-administration per se
* Interactive features like clickable definitions are used rarely by *Rs*
  - Even small differences in (already low) effort affect *Rs'* willingness to use interactive features
  - Progress Indicators seem to backfire, increasing breakoffs when they communicate that *R* still has a long way to go
