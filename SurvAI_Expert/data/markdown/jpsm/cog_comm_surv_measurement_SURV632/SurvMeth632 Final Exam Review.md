**Class 7: Context Effects in Attitude Measurement**

* **What is the difference between temporarily accessible information and chronically accessible information?**
  * Temporarily accessible information is currently active information in a respondent's mind due to recent exposure from the survey questions themselves, a recent conversation, or external factors like current events. This information can significantly influence responses, particularly attitude questionnaires, as it provides immediate context and relevance for the respondent. Chronically accessible information is information that is readily available in a respondent's minds due to frequent use or strong association. This includes beliefs values, such as political ideology. This type of information can also impact responses to attitudinal questions as it serves as a contextual filter used by respondents to interpret questions.

* **What are the different types of context effects?**
  * There are two types of context effects: Assimilation effects can occur when answering attitudinal questions and the respondent uses information from earlier questions, which this is known as a priming effect, and contrast effects can occur when respondents exclude recent information when judging their response.
  * An example of an assimilation effect is when some respondent as asked a general life satisfaction question and later asked a more specific satisfaction question which some respondent may recall how they answered the previous question to map back to the recent question.
  * On the other hand, contrast effects are divided into comparison based which occur when respondents compare two items, and subtraction based effects which are when respondents cognitively subtract an item's characteristics from another item. In the Schwartz article, when respondents were asked to exclude the specific satisfaction item(s) from the general the correlation between these responses were no longer significant, suggesting that respondents are able to mentally subtract items but may need instructions to do so.

* **What is the relationship between attitude accessibility and context effects**
  * In a study that measure response times to answering questions, respondents that did not change their response were considered non-movers and had crystallized preconceived attitudes. This meant that for these respondents they were able to

* **How does the age/working memory of respondents affect context effects?**
  * As individuals get older, things like assimilation effect cannot be held in working memory as much; therefore, older respondents’ working memory declines with age and are less affected by context effects.

* **How does the presence of buffer items between the context and target affect context effects?**
  * Buffers are used in surveys to provide unrelated items between related items. A study that asked respondents about attitudes towards the KKK and adding buffer items found that assimilation affects were minimized for that target item. This is because having respondents think about other topics allows them to disconnect the general or specific target item previous administered.

This was only the case for the frequency ratings and not the caloric intake items.

* **What cultural variable influences context effects?**
  * Cultural variables that influence context effects are Collectivist and individualism where different cultures can be reluctant to disclose information while others more open. East Asian cultures are collectivist and pay closer attention to the survey context. As an example, they are likely to subtract their response on an academic satisfaction question from a general life satisfaction question. Alternatively, respondents from individualistic cultures are less attentive to the survey context and were less likely to subtract the satisfaction questions. This context effect for the individualistic respondents leads to a strong correlation between related items thus contributing to measurement error.

**Class 9: Interpreting and Coding Open Responses**

* **What are some different models of everyday classification?**
    * An exemplar model classifies new objects, such as a sparrow, and classifies it into a pre-constructed category, such as “birds” A prototype model classifies based on the abstract features of the sparrow, for instance it has wings and lives in a nest, so it must be a type of bird. Both the exemplar and prototype models are probabilistic because the objects can also be compared against other classes of membership. The classical model is deterministic containing rules that help match a new object to a group membership category.

* **What is an example of a basic level of categorization?**
    * Coders prefer intermediate abstractions that are basic levels of categorization. As an example, if a question asked “what are you sitting on?” An advance level response would specific such as a “Razer gaming desk chair” while a lower level response would be “plastic and foam,” yet a basic level would be “a chair.”

* **What are some techniques from Malhotra and Krosnick to use cognitive psychology to updating coding schemes?**

<!-- page 7 -->

* Malhotra had coders classify new biotech job descriptions to extend an occupational standard classification system using a method commonly used in psychology called Q-sort, where objects are cognitively categorized accordingly as well as rate them.

* **How can socially derived coding rules harm the quality of coding?**
    * When responses are abstract, coders tend to create their own set of coding rules that are undocumented and which result in lower rater agreement and reliability of classification code.

* **How can strict or rigid criteria lead to misclassification?**
    * When coding rules tend to be strict, coders may miss classifying a correct response such as in the study that examined strict rules for coding political questions. When respondents provided acceptable responses such as "court" but left out "supreme" these were coded as incorrect even through they were correct given the strict. This of course would influence the study's results given the reduced number correct classifications.

* **How do the length and difficulty of descriptions affect coding?**
    * The Conrad study on occupations found that coders tend to disagree more as the descriptions get longer and more ambiguous. This affect is modified by the complexity of words in the description. Another study modified the words and length and found that for simple descriptions, there was less agreement among coders when the description got longer. Moreover, for descriptions with harder words, there was more agreement for longer words, suggesting that when the description is technical more words may help coders classify it.

* **What are the trade-offs between using field and office coding staff?**
    * One major advantage for using field coders is that there tends to be more of them and tap into their expertise since they are closer to the respondents. Because there are more of them, the work load is smaller. Office coders on the other hand offer a different set of expertise that is also beneficial to the coding workflow. The trade-off is that is is time intensive and expensive to train so many field coders. In regards to coder inter coder reliability, there is not much difference between office or field coders once trained. The biggest gains comes from having a smaller work load and less within coder variability.

* **How does increasing the number of coders or decreasing the workload for coders influence the precision of estimates from coding?**

* We can use the variance inflation of coder clusters equation to assess standard errors. The Sturgis study used this equation and found that smaller work loads has a large impact on minimizing the standard errors, which will affect confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. Therefore, more coders equate smaller work loads and thus reducing the variance inflation metric.

**Class 10: Technology Mediated Responding**

Multitasking in this way can lead to interviewers missing utterances from the respondents that may indicate confusion or the need for clarification. In one study, interviewers that had to divide their attention between technology and the respondent, they made contact with them about a third of the time.

**Class 11: Survey Satisficing**

* **What is survey satisficing?**
  * When respondents provide acceptable responses without optimizing through cognitive effort this is considered satisficing. Straightlining or mental coin flips are forms of satisficing.

* **What are examples of weak satisficing behavior?**
  * Settling for the first response, such as primacy, or recency as well as being agreeable with the survey questions is a form of weak satisficing. Weak satisfiing assumes that the respondent went through the stages of the cognitive response process less optimally

* **What are some examples of strong satisficing behavior?**

<!-- page 10 -->

* If respondents skip the entire cognitive process this is a form of strong satisficing. Usually this can occur when the task for the question is difficult

* **What are some respondent characteristics that influence satisficing?**
  * Some respondent characteristics influencing satisficing include a respondent's ability to answer a question which tends to be an attribute that can be fixed or vary by context. An example of ability is cognitive sophistication which despite a difficult task being high on this attribute may lead to less satisficing if any. Another characteristic is a respondent's motivation to answer a survey question which can also be affected by an interviewer. The task is also a characteristic and can be impacted by surveys that are long and induce burden,

* **How does the task influence satisficing?**
  * When questions contain complicated words that need clarification or definitions and these are not readily available to respondents it may influence satisficing. Response options may also influence satisficing such as having too many responses to process or not being able to retrieve the information required to make a judgment.

* **How does mode influence satisficing?**
  * Modes that have visual response cards tend to influence primacy effects, while telephone surveys tend to influence recency effects. It is also much harder to straightline in text surveys or even move quickly through the questions. Self administer modes tend to put less preasure on respondents to produce an answer and less likely to satisfice, particularly if the question is difficult.

* **How is age related to speeding in a questionnaire?**
  * Zhang found that 18 to 34 were notorious speeders which is an indicator for satisficing. As respondents became older, they were less likely to speed through the survey. Also, respondents that speed through the first few questions will likely speed questions at the end of the survey.

* **How is speeding related to straightlining?**
  * Respondents that selected the same response, called straightlining, in a grid format is related to speeding since it doesn't take long to respond with one answer for all questions on the same page without reading the questions.

* **What are some methods for reducing speeding?**
  * A study found that providing prompts when respondents are going to fast through the survey tends to be effective in slowing them down which may reduce

  * In a study that conducted latent class analysis to classify respondents into satisficers and optimizers found that traits were the main driver for satisficing regardless of ability or motivation. **Class 12: Cross-cultural surveys**

* **What is the difference between adaptation and translation?**
  * Adapting a survey relates to modifying parts of the survey to fit the intended audience. An example of an adaptation for a question about height from a US population to a British population would be changing out feet for meters in the responses. Another example is when asking in a US population about coffee consumption, and adapting the same question to a British sample while substituting coffee for tea. Whereas a translation is taking a survey question and responses from one source language and translating it to the target language.

* **What is the TRAPD method of translation?**
  * The first step in the TRAPD method is translation by several translators, taking the source survey language and translating it to the target language. This is followed by the review step where one or more reviewer examines the translation quality. In the next phase there is an adjudicator that mediates between the reviewer and translator and makes a final decision which then gets pre-tested in the next phase. This process is iterative and can go back to the translation phase as many times as needed. Finally this process should always be documented in the last stage.

* **What is the difference between literal translation and functional equivalence?**
  * For some surveys literal translation is a good starting point, yet there are times that the intended target language does not have a translation for a word, or worst, a phrase in the question does not mean the same. Functional translation takes into account pragmatics for which people use language in different ways and attempts to produce a question or set of responses that are equivalent across the source and target languages. As an example, in Hebrew “disagree nor agree” does not have a literal translation and a functional equivalence must be committed, in this case, “in the middle.” This reduces the likelihood that the translation bias is contributing to measurement error.

* **What are some different dimensions along which cultures vary?**
  * Hofesteded’s work on cross cultural psychology to this date is still relevant, and used in the world values survey. He proposed six orthogonal cultural dimensions that impact individual psychological processes.
  * The more common dimension used in cross cultural research is individualism versus collectivism, which is the degree in which some members prioritize their individualistic values, such as personal goals and achievements, while other members in a collectivist system prioritizes group harmony and interdependence thinking. An example of collectivist thinking comes from the word “ubuntu” from the tribes in South Africa which translates to “I am because we are,” which highlights a strong sense of community as the foundation of a society.
  * Masculinity versus femininity is another popular studied cultural dimension when examining how gender roles are prescribed in a society. For instance, in some Latin American cultures, “machismo,” a strong sense of masculinity, is valued and often prescribed among males.
  * Power distance seems to be common in the political science literature more so than the behavioral sciences but posits how much members of a society accept the distribution of social power. Sociologist tend to study this from the perspective of social stratification of gender, race, or social class which theorizes on the hierarchy of how power in one culture is distributed.
  * Uncertainty avoidance is about how much members of a society tolerate ambiguity. We see this in anthropology studies in how some cultural groups think of the afterlife as well as other unpredictable situations and view them as the will of God, or deterministic and out of the hands of the individual.
  * Long and short term orientation explores how cultural groups think of time. This can lead to how much individuals in one culture plan for life events later in their life course, or live for the moment.
  * Indulgence versus restraint impacts how different cultural groups engage in immediate gratification or delay it.

* **How does the individualism/collectivism dimension influence different stages of the response process?**
  * The cognitive response process is influenced by the culture in every stage of the process. In the comprehension stage, culture can modify the pragmatic meaning of words or how the context is used. In the German and Chinese study on academic satisfaction we saw that the Chinese students paid more attention to the context and were not so much affected by the specific satisfaction priming the general satisfaction question, compared to the German students. In the retrieval stage, the cognitive process of how individuals store and retrieve information can be modified by their culture, for instance, some life events are more pronounced in some cultural than others. For instance, in some Latin American cultures, a girls 15 year birthday is highly anticipated and celebrated which the events surround this day are perhaps more encoded than a 15 year American girl. We also see in some studies that respondents from collectivist cultures retrieve group based events more often, while individualist respondents can more easily retrieve information on their internal state. The Schwartz study on ratings of health among English and Spanish speakers highlights how culture affects the judgment stage. When asked about specific health questions prior to general health, Spanish speakers rated their health lower than in the reverse order, suggesting that Spanish speakers interpret health more broadly. In the response stage, culture impacts what should be reported and what should be kept private. Respondents from collectivist cultures value group harmony which also means saving face and my not be completely honest when responding to sensitive questions as they may perceive that hiding the truth is more honorable. Whereas individualistic respondents values perceiving themselves I a positive light and may modify their responses to sensitive questions leading to social desirability bias. In cultures that are future oriented, they may strongly consider the long term impact of their response and edit as needed.

* **How can translations cause heterogeneity in reporting?**
  * Respondents from different cultures may use the response scales differently, or in other words the psychometric thresholds may be inequivalent across cultures, as seen in the study on pain ratings where Chinese respondents used the fair response different from the Swedish respondents. This leads to heterogeneity in the estimates that contribute to measurement error.
