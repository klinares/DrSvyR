Review for Midterm

* Measurement error:
  * ME is the difference between what respondents report and the true value.
* The Response Process Theory
  * The response process theory is an iterative mental process of four phases that respondents go through while answering a survey item.
  * The **comprehension** phase involves the understanding of the meaning of words in a question, and a lack of understanding of what the item writer intended leads to response issues, or measurement error.
    * **Semantics** deal with meanings of words or logic and can lead to ambiguity.
      * In the example in class on “The best way to prevent cancer is to catch it early” it was unclear whether the statement meant that testing early can prevent cancer, or catching it early can. In this case, “catch” carries more than one meaning.
      * Mapping issues, for example “did you have more than one job last week?” where respondent is not sure what constitutes as a job or what to exclude.
    * Respondents make a **pragmatic** inference about words in a question in terms of their non-literal meaning. As an example, the word “children” can include different age ranges for different people.
      * **Clark and Schober** outline five principles of language use as they claim that in a survey interview, interviewers and respondents engages in a social process. Principle of speaker’s meaning involves a respondent recognizing what the question is asking.
        * They also suggest that surveys are different from everyday conversations where there is pressure for respondent to respond consistently
        * **Common ground** is a shared understanding between the interviewer and respondent and as they go through the interview, respondents use the context to achieve grounding. **Grounding** is a mutual understanding between the interviewer and respondent, where respondents if confused can seek clarification. Common ground leads to understanding questions to provide meaningful responses.
          * Ex. Respondents record in working memory previous answers survey questions to infer on the meaning of current questions and how to answer them, such as the marital and general satisfaction example.
        * **Cooperative principle** dictates that through conversations, people generally cooperate with each other to achieve a common goal, and Grice proposed 4 maxims for how people achieve goals.
          * The **quantity maxim** is about only contributing what is necessary, **quality** is about contributing what is truthful, **relation** is about contributing relevant information, and **manner** means being clear.
          * ex. I just ran out of gas, there is a gas station down the street.
        * **Principle of Utterances** respondents produce utterances, metacognitive cues, for interviewers to either clarify a question or find common ground. If there is grounding, interviewers will help respondents achieve the goal of responding to the survey question.
    * Standardized survey interviews should minimize interviewer variance and allow for comparative responses across respondents. It carries limitations such as increasing the chances of respondents misunderstand questions, because extracting meaning of ambiguous words requires going back and forth between the interviewer and respondent.

* Conversational interviews can improve response accuracy by achieving grounding by creating a space for respondents to get more clarification on questions. This type of interviews can be useful for sensitive or complex surveys.

* **Retrieval** is about recalling information from long term memory, and memory is a representation of an event, but we can sometimes forget events thus introducing error in retrieval, which is the process of constructing information from long term memory to an active state.
    * Factors that lead to recall error are incomplete encoding, similarity of events, changes in memories over time, passage of time.
    * Different kinds of memory include long term memory such episodic, and working memory, which constructs memory.
    * Episodic, **autobiographic memory** are periods of ones life and these memories are salient to the individual.
    * Three models of memory
        * Kolodner – event memory are organized almost as a file cabinet structure, but instead of alphabetical, they are indexed by similarities in events or episodic life periods.
        * Conway **multilayered model memory** is organized by lifetime periods, and it is easier to recall events that are closer to the beginning or end of a period, such as when you graduated college, or your first adult job. Recall is better with life time periods rather than calendar years, and helps to retrieve events.
            * Specific knowledge about an event is recalled with a life period associated with it as well as nearby events, which the more similar they are the harder it is to recall specifics. Ex. While I was an undergrad, I was also in the military.
        * Thomsen’s Autobio periods (AP), **hierarchical memory** is organized with details nested under life periods which is called life period thinking that is influenced by socio-cultural processes, periodic experiences, and goals.
            * At the top level there are life domains that are close to developmental stages in one’s life course. Below are individual events, such as one’s first job which are generic memories that help capture features of events. At the bottom are sensory information, details about specifics in an event.
            * Recall of events is more accurate when search involves autobio and lifetime periods.
        * **Lee’s** study about parents recall on their children’s vaccine record showed us that sometimes it is not a retrieval issue but an encoding problem where we do not record the event in memory. Using event calenders could help a little bit, but if the events are not properly encoded than there isn’t much to retrieve.
        * **Encoding specificity** when we make respondents picture the context in which they were in when the event occurred, as long as it is encoded, they can recall it.
        * **Techniques to improve recall** include giving more time to respond, decomposing meaning breaking events down, event history calendars, and cues.
            * Retrieval cues about what happened rather than when or to whom are better cues for retrieving event. Retrieval of location is better than the activity. Time stamps are not useful because that is not how we remember events, unless it is salient.
        * **Reminiscence bump** shows that people have better recall of events between 10-30, this could be due to so many developmental stages/phases that occur make certain events salient.
        * Age of events impact recall, which is correlated with our age and as we get older or move further away in time from an event we begin to loose the ability to recall it.

* Forward telescoping is time compression and the most common form of this type of process when answering time reference period questions is forward telescoping, where respondents are more likely to include in the reference period events that occurred outside.
  * Theories
    * For longer reference periods, more events outside of the period are reported on since time compresses.
    * Knowledge of events is likely to lead to a respondent remembering the event “as if it were yesterday” and incorrectly can report them into the reference period, which is called the **accessibility principle**.
    * **Variance theories** of telescoping assumes that error is unbiased and greater for older events.
      * Unbiased error means that events have an equal chance of being moved forward or backwards in time, and that this error is normally distributed around the actual date. Therefore, the probability of incorrectly forward placing an event because that occurred back in time have more error.
* **Seam effect** Changes between waves are larger than changes from month to month in between waves, thus introducing error in longitudinal surveys. This is because people forget, except for the previous month before the survey. Secondly, the constant wave responding means that respondents carry values from month to month since if they don’t know the answer they still have to give one.
  * One way to prevent is to remind respondents how they responded previously.

* **Estimation/Judgment**: Since we have imperfect recall, we still have to quantify events in terms of occurrence, duration, elapsed time (how long since the event?), and temporal frequency (how long are the episodes?).
  * **Duration** is affected by how people perceive the event or felt about it.
  * Some events are harder to retrieve based on accessibility of available information, so people tend to use strategies to estimate a rate.
    * **Metric and mapping framework:**
      * **Metric knowledge** quantitatively derives a rate (mean or range) as an estimate because we do not store in memory a tally of how many times something has occurred.
        * Quantifying a rate can be affected by things like scale response, how infrequent the event is, cultural and private events, and access to the information.
        * ex. I exercise on Mondays and Fridays, twice a week, so in the past year I have exercised about 100 times depending on whether I was sick or not.
      * Mapping knowledge is a qualitative strategy that encodes relative frequencies.
        * Heuristics are shortcuts to resolve an answer using accessible information. However, for rare events we may under report because we may have forgotten that it actually occurred. These mental shortcuts are useful when computing an estimate becomes more demanding.
          * **Availability heuristic** are about using an visual representation that accessible in working memory to derive an answer. Example, it is easier to recall famous names on a list than non-famous.

* **Representativeness heuristic** are about using strategies that classify similar events as in the Tversky study of trade profiles that sounded like engineers were classified as such based on what respondents perceived were attributes of this trade.
  * **Anchor and adjust heuristics** starts with an estimate and makes adjustments accordingly to map the final answer.
* **Decomposing** a question into parts can sometimes make recall easier; however, the Belli article showed that for non-distinct events such as local calls respondents overestimated. In this case decomposition added more error.
* **Behavioral frequency** question strategy for quantifying a behavior is to recall the events and count them, and this is called **enumerating** events. However, with longer periods of events enumeration, or recall of events goes down, and the rate is underestimated.
  * This strategy is rarely used for “how often questions” and more so for “how many times.”
  * The **Burton and Blair paper** showed that events that were frequent and similar led to enumeration underestimating and rates overestimating.
  * For frequent behaviors or similar people use rates, while for infrequent events or distinct enumeration is used.
* The **multiple strategy perspective** are non numeric strategies used for coming up with numerical answers when enumeration is difficult and there is no accessible information.
  * **General impression strategy** is likely to overestimate events that are regular and similar using heuristics.
* Older adults are more likely to use impression strategy.
* Brown using diaries to report frequency of activities and emotions showed people used available knowledge about themselves, such as whether they were happy people, to answer to emotional state during an activity.
  * People used memory strategies to report on activity frequent, and for emotions they used knowledge about themselves.
