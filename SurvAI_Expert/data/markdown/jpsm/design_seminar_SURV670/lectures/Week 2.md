# Statistical Consulting in Academia: At Michigan – A Time of Change

## CSCAR Update

Research universities do amazing things. They save lives, create opportunity, and improve quality of life for people all over our state, across the nation, and around the world. The Office of the Vice President for Research provides a wide range of essential services to principal investigators of externally funded research grants to help the University of Michigan do groundbreaking, life-saving work in so many areas.

As you may also know, research universities like ours operate in an increasingly challenging environment. Terminations, investigations, and new regulations are just some of the new realities that we must manage. In light of funding challenges associated with these changes, the research office had to review its entire portfolio of services to clarify the things for which we are primarily responsible at this moment. Part of this review examined benchmarking statistical and data services across campus and listening to members of our community who offer or receive these services in different ways. As a result of this review, the office made the decision to wind down its financial support of CSCAR over the coming year.

In past decades, CSCAR was the only campus source for statistical consulting. That is no longer the case. So, since earlier this year, we have been coordinating with campus partners and others to identify ways for participating units to share the costs of CSCAR or to take it over. We have not been successful. While we are able to offer continuing support to researchers currently working with CSCAR on grants for the duration of those grants, we have turned our focus to finding alternative options for others to find statistical and coding advice. There are many sources available on campus and online. In the coming weeks, we will roll out detailed information on how to find them. We are grateful to everyone involved in this effort.

Making decisions like this is difficult. At the same time, these decisions are essential for the research office to manage challenges to funding environments that have arisen in recent months — and to focus on areas where OVPR has the primary responsibility to protect and advance the university's research mission.

**Michael Traugott** <[email]> Jan 12 (6 days ago)
to Ford

Ford - Thanks for your email. There are three major factors that can affect the accuracy of a pre-election poll estimate of an election outcome: the quality of the sample, the weighting algorithm, and the likely voter model. If you look at the Bloomberg Politics Poll Decoder (https://www.bloomberg.com/politics/graphics/2016-poll-decoder/), you will get an idea of the variance in the samples (allowing for weighting) in the support for Clinton and Trump among various groups that supported them as well as those that weren't leaning either way. For example, we know a lot about the gender gap, and it should have favored Clinton. Across a group of late polls, her average advantage among women was about 12.6 percentage points, but in one sample it was 17 pp and in the lowest it was 6 pp. A second issue is weighting, and the standard approach is to weight to know population characteristics for which there is an external source like the Census. These factors include age, race, sex, and region. But there are other factors that some pollsters use like past voting behavior or party identification. There are no equivalent external distributions on which to base such weights, and issues can arise (see the example of the USC Dornsife panel weighting issues).

The third issue is that voting is a two-step process in the US: you have to get registered and then get a ballot. Turnout in the US is slightly greater than 50% in the US among the voting eligible population (around 59% in 2016 according to McDonald - http://www.electproject.org/2016g). So here is where the secret source comes in, because there is no standard model for estimating likelihood to vote. Usually the models involve a variety of factors: self-reported registration status, interest in the campaign (a survey measure), past voting behavior, and self-described likelihood of voting (subject to strong social desirability influences). What is the reference point for pollsters using LV models? Typically it is the last or recent elections (I just decided to add some slides from various post-election talks I gave; I think they are probably self-explanatory in the context of this email). Obama's elections turned out not to be very good predictors of voting because the Democrats lost votes and vote share in 2012 and 2016. The Republicans may have turned out people who didn't vote in 2008 or 2012 but did in 2004. We also saw for the first time a different turnout pattern in rural areas (higher) than in urban areas (lower) that caught many political observers by surprise after the fact. I have also come to believe since 2012 that he candidates' emphasis on the battleground states and the differentially higher turnout there compared to the rest of the country may mean that they should be treated as a separate stratum with a different likely voter model than the rest of the country.

Having said all of this, the national estimates of the popular vote - after all the votes were counted - were very accurate. The problem was lower quality estimation in the states. And the aggregators and modelers who used state level polls uniformly got in trouble. Beyond this, there is no accounting for the Clinton campaign not advertising in Wisconsin or visiting there; a similar question about Michigan.

Now back to you original question about the use of precincts. They are by definition aggregates, so you can only make inferences about voting behavior in them when they are homogeneous. Comparing them over time, you could detect some patterns by size of place or perhaps race/ethnicity. But they won't tell you anything about voting patterns by gender or age, for example, nor about people who held different attitudes about government or specific policies. Only survey data at the individual level can enlighten you there.

Let me know if this helps or if you have additional questions. Mike

### The political communication project studies how impressions of political candidates are formed by focusing on the role of media representations of them in conjunction with discussions on social media.

There are two distinct components to this research: an analysis of elite behavior in the press, and an examination of how news coverage and Twitter commentary affect citizens' impressions of the candidates.

### Elite behavior in the media

In the analysis of elite behavior, researchers focus on the news content in the mainstream media for two weeks leading up to specific campaign events as well as two weeks after. They examine the construction of the news through journalists' interactions with each other, prior to, during and after important campaign events through Twitter, the platform journalists now see as an essential part of their job.

**b. Do we include PCR-neg patients in addition to the positives, or only the positives? There is a constraint on the N in our registry and some don’t want to “waste” resources on the negatives. I think it could be valuable in the following way. Tests were and are being done on patients who present with non-specific symptoms suggestive of COVID-19. Comparing positives vs negatives would give insight into the differential diagnosis as we would see what diagnoses resulted for all the people with negative PCR tests. It would also be valuable to compare hospitalization rates and fatality rates between the positives and negatives to get an idea of how much “excess mortality” is associated with COVID-19. So, 2 decisions here – do we include any PCR-negative patients, and if so, in what ratio of positives-to-negatives, bearing in mind that statistical power will be needed for queries about susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection, but not severity of disease.**

This memo is going to be a bit free-flowing, as I try to understand as best as I can the issues involving the sample design for the XXX Market Share Survey.

Although the unit of analysis is the package, as a practical matter you will have to sample “shippers.” I use that term in quotes because this is a somewhat ill-defined population that consists of all a) persons who sent small packages (defined as 0-150 lbs) during the past year, and b) of all organizations that sent small packages during the past year, subdivided down to the unit at which records are tracked as to the details of the small package shipping decisions, including the choice of the vendor that sends a given small package. Persons, of course, can appear in both populations, either representing themselves in the personal life, if you will, or their organizations in their work, professional, or personal life.

It is relatively easy to deal with the former; given the ubiquity of small package shipment, I might simply suggest a random-digit-dial survey. You might want to consider asking about the past month, however, given that personal shipments might be considerably less common that organizational shipments.

For the latter, the trick will be obtaining a sampling frame that corresponds with the population. Marrying the conceptual and practical here is difficult. As a start I imagine that, in principle at least, you can obtain a sampling frame of businesses stratified by SIC or NAICS code and size. You sample from within these strata at random with a known probability of selection. The probability of selection can differ across strata or even within strata (for example you might want to select a few major retailers, particularly mail order retailers, with probability 1), as long as you maintain a record of what the probability of selection is. Once you have a business, you may need to have a second or even third round of sampling within the facility (this will probably have to be at random) if there is no centralized recording of small package shipments within the business. For example, if you sample LL Bean, it may be that they can tell you to the nearest dollar the nine-figure amounts of shipping costs by shipper, but at the University of Pennsylvania, it is likely that these data are kept, at best, at a department or division level; that would require getting a list of departments, sampling n out of N of them at random, and retaining this second-stage sampling fraction record for construction of future weights.

To construct the sampling weights for the $i$th shipper in $j$th business in the $h$th stratum, let $n_h$ be the number of business sampled from the $h$th stratum at random from $N_h$ businesses, and $m_{jh}$ be the number of shippers in the $j$th business, drawn at random from the $M_{jh}$ shippers determined to exist in the $j$th business. Then the sampling design weight would be

$$d_{ijh} = \frac{M_{jh} N_h}{m_{jh} n_h} \text{ .}$$

If there are known control totals $X_h$ (e.g., total sales, etc.) within a given stratum, one can construct calibrated or “poststratified” weights $w_{ijh}$ such that $\sum_i \sum_j w_{ijh} x_{ijh} = X_h$ using generalized regression or generalized raking estimators. UPS may have some specific control totals of interest at the SIC-level to which they may wanted your weight totals to correspond.
