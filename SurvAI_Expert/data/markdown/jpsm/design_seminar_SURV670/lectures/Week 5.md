# There Are Three Criteria for Establishing a Causal Relationship

---

1. **Covariation**
   * **When the values on one variable change, so do values on the other**
   * **Variables CHANGE TOGETHER, they are CO-RELATED**
2. **Temporal Order**
   * **Changes on the independent variable precede changes on the dependent variable in time. X is the cause of Y and not the reverse.**
3. **Elimination of rival hypotheses**
   * **Other potential causes are accounted for and ruled out. PLAUSIBLE ALTERNATIVE HYPOTHESES are eliminated**

# How to Examine Assertions or Claims

1. State the question/problem simply: Is there a hypothesis?
2. Identify the key concepts
3. Identify the hypotheses that link the concepts together
4. Look at the explanations
   * \*Are causal relationships stated or implied?
   * \*What kind of causal relationships make sense?
   * \*Are any important variables left out, i.e., intervening, conditional, or explanatory?
5. Are the necessary conditions for causality satisfied?
   * Covariation
   * Temporal order
   * Elimination of Rival or Plausible Alternative Hypotheses (ERH or PAH)

# How Researchers Deal with Systematic Measurement Error

1. Researchers (Assistants) are blind to the Hypothesis
2. Subjects are blind to the Hypothesis
3. Both researchers and subjects are blind to the Hypothesis (double blind)
4. Careful design of measures and checking against other known measures of the same concept

Searching for systematic error and assuring ourselves that it is minimized involves the notion of VALIDITY

# Design of Experiments

The experimental design begins in the laboratory under controlled conditions (physical science):

This means that the researcher

1) Establishes the conditions that are the equivalent of the independent variable
2) Observes the value of the dependent variable before and after the independent variable is applied
3) Knows that the independent variable precedes the change in the value of the dependent variable

# How Do We Evaluate the Quality of Our Design?

## DESIGN VALIDITY comes in two forms: Internal and External.

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Internal Validity** | **1. Reliable and valid measures**<br>**2. Establish causality:**<br>**Covariation, temporal order,**<br>**Elimination of Rival Hypotheses** |
| **External Validity** | **3. Generalize from our study to**<br>**the real world** |

**Note: Design validity *incorporates* measurement validity**

<!-- page 42 -->

# Internal Validity

---

1. The conclusions drawn from the analysis accurately reflect what went on during the period of observation
2. The conditions for establishing causality were fulfilled

Threats: Alternative hypotheses/ other possible independent variables, poor measurement, can't determine temporal order, improper data analysis

<!-- page 43 -->

# Threats to Internal Validity

* **Mortality**: Did certain types of subjects drop out of the study?
* **History**: Other events between T1 and T2?
* **Maturation**: Did the subjects get older and change over time?
* **Instrumentation**: Changes in measurement
* **Regression to the mean**: Extreme scores are likely to be less extreme when measured again
* **Testing**: Could the act of the pretest affect the post-test measurement (sensitization)?

<!-- page 44 -->

# External Validity

---

**The conclusions drawn from the analysis apply in the “real world”**

**Threats:**
1. **The subjects are different than the population you are trying to understand**
2. **The setting for the data collection is not “naturalistic”**

<!-- page 45 -->

# Threats to External Validity

---

1. **The units of analysis are different than the ones to which the researcher is trying to generalize in the “real world.”**

   **Are college sophomores the same as “real people”?**

   **NOTE: Only a problem if they differ in ways that would affect the relationship!**

<!-- page 46 -->

---

2. The setting in which the observations were made is not like the “typical” setting in which the relationship might arise in the “real world”

   Watching television in a group with strangers in a lab setting versus watching at home

   Again: Would the setting affect the relationship being studied?

# There are two kinds of plausible alternative hypotheses:

---

1. Those that could exist on some theoretical or conceptual basis, i.e. be reasonably known in advance.

   These can be dealt with by including another variable in the study **(PAH)**

2. Those that are unknown but might exist

   These are dealt with in experiments through **RANDOMIZATION**, i.e., making all other things equal

An experiment including appropriate randomization deals effectively with PAH's, especially the “unknown” kind
