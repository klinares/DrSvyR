# Blood lead levels, ALAD gene polymorphisms, and mortality.

van Bemmel DM$^1$, Li Y, McLean J, Chang MH, Dowling NF, Graubard B, Rajaraman P.

## Author information

## Abstract

**BACKGROUND:** Previous analyses from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III) have found that elevated blood lead levels may be associated with cardiovascular mortality, cancer mortality, and all-cause mortality. The 5-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase (ALAD) G177C genetic polymorphism (rs 1800435) affects lead toxicokinetics and may alter the adverse effects of lead exposure. We examined whether the ALAD G177C single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) affects the relationship between lead and mortality.

**METHODS:** We analyzed a subset of 3349 genotyped NHANES III participants at least 40 years of age. Using Cox proportional hazards regression, we estimated the relative risk of all-cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality by ALAD genotype, and by blood lead levels (<5 $\mu$g/dL vs. $\ge$5 $\mu$g/dL). We also tested whether the ALAD genotype modified the relationship between blood lead level and mortality.

**RESULTS:** The adjusted overall relative risk for participants with the variant ALAD genotype was decreased for all-cause mortality (hazards ratio = 0.68; [95% confidence interval = 0.50-0.93]) compared with persons having the common GG genotype. There was some suggestion that higher lead levels were associated with cancer mortality (1.48 [0.92-2.38]). We observed no convincing interaction effect between ALAD genotype and blood lead level on mortality risk.

**CONCLUSION:** The ALAD genotype may be associated with decreased mortality from all causes and from cancer. This association does not seem to be affected by lead exposure.

# Complex Sample Survey Data: Probability Samples

* **Probability sample design**:
  - Each population element has a known, non-zero selection probability
  - Properly weighted, sample estimates are unbiased or nearly unbiased for the corresponding population statistic.
  - Variance of sample statistics can be estimated from the sample data (measurability)

* **Simple random sample (SRS):** A probability sample in which each element has an independent and equal chance of being selected for observation. Closest population sampling analog to independently and identically distributed (iid) data.

11

<!-- page 12 -->

# Complex Sample Survey Data: “Complex” Designs

* “Complex sample”:
  * A probability sample developed using sampling procedures such as **stratification, clustering and weighting** designed to <u>improve statistical efficiency, reduce costs or improve precision for subgroup analyses</u> relative to SRS
  * Unbiased estimates with measurable sampling error are still possible
  * Independence and identical distribution of observations (i.i.d.), equal probabilities of selection may no longer hold

# Where are Complex Sample Designs Used?

* Complex sample designs are the rule and not the exception in sample-based studies in the Social Sciences, Epidemiology, Public Health, Agriculture, Natural Resources and many other scientific fields

* Common designs
  - Area probability samples of household populations
  - Multi-stage samples of schools, classes and students
  - Stratified samples of businesses, hospitals

* Other designs
  - Dual-frame and other samples in agriculture
  - RDD telephone samples
  - Natural resource samples (time and location samples)

# NHANES SAMPLE DESIGN

* Primary stage:
  - The PSUs are mostly single counties.
  - Stratify PSUs by region and MSA status.
  - Select PSUs by probability proportionate to estimated size (PPeS) sampling (a few selected with certainty).

# Sampling Design for Population Studies

* **Sample Plan** – framework and method for selecting the observational units
  * *Probability sampling*: Hansen, Hurwitz, Madow (1953); Kish (1965)
  * *Model-assisted sampling*: Sarndal, Swensson, Wretman (1992)

* **Estimation and Inference**
  * *Design-based* (repeated sampling - **focus of this course**)
    We think conceptually about selecting samples over and over according to a specified design. An estimator (for population total, say) is evaluated from each sample. The properties of the estimator are computed by averaging over the set of possible samples Base on the process of selecting the sample
  * *Model-based*
    A model is used that is intended to describe the structure that exists in the population. This structure is the same regardless of how the sample is selected. Inferences are made using the probability distribution associated with the model
    Based on the underlying structure in the population

# Statistical Inference (Model-based)

1) Incorporates a probability model, $f(y|x, \theta)$ for the variables.
   a) ex: $Y(1),\dots,Y(n)$ are independent observations from a standard normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$.
   b) ex: coin flip – Bernouli trial
      $P(\text{Head}) = .50$
      $T(\text{Tail}) = .50$

2) Goal is to estimate the parameters, $\theta$, of $f(y|x,\theta)$. Assumes no finite limit to the process that generates the sample Ys.

3) Estimators, tests of hypothesis, confidence intervals derived on the basis of the probability model.

Extremely powerful <u>if</u> the probability model is correct.

   ex:
$$F = \frac{GM_1M_2}{R^2} + \text{error}$$

# “Design-Based” Inference from Sample Surveys

* Goal is inference about finite population parameter $\theta$
* Inference is based on the expected distribution of sample estimates, **not** on a probability model, $f(y|x,\theta)$ for $Ys$;
* A generic form for confidence interval for a population parameter $\theta$

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
(1) & (2) & (3) \\
\hat{\theta} \pm & t_{df, 1-\alpha/2} & \cdot se(\hat{\theta})
\end{array}
$$

Example: $\bar{y} \pm t_{\infty, .975} \cdot se(\bar{y})$

$$
30.0 \pm 1.96 \times (1.30)
$$

$$
(27.45, 32.54)
$$

* $t_{df}(p)$ is the value of the Student – $t$ distribution with $df$ degrees of freedom cutting off tail-probability $p/2$. Assumes normality of the sampling distribution of $\hat{\theta}$
* $se(\hat{\theta})$ the estimated standard error (square root of the variance) of the sample estimate, $\hat{\theta}$. Special computational formulas and/or methods are needed.

# Concept of the Design Effect

$$D^2(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{SE(\hat{\theta})_{complex}^2}{SE(\hat{\theta})_{srs}^2} = \frac{Var(\hat{\theta})_{complex}}{Var(\hat{\theta})_{srs}}$$

Where:

$D^2(\hat{\theta}) =$ the design effect for the sample estimate, $\hat{\theta}$

$Var(\hat{\theta})_{complex} =$ the complex sample design variance of $\hat{\theta}$;

and

$Var(\hat{\theta})_{srs} =$ the simple random sample variance of $\hat{\theta}$

26

<!-- page 27 -->

# Design Effects for Complex Samples

$$
D^2(\hat{\theta}) \approx 1 + f(G_{strat}, L_{cluster}, L_{weighting}),
$$

where:

$G_{strat} =$ the relative gain in precision from stratified sampling compared to SRS

$L_{cluster} =$ the relative loss of precision due to clustered selection of sample elements

$L_{weighting} =$ the relative loss of precision due to unequal weighting for sample elements

# Finite Population Correction (*fpc*)

* Sampling without replacement (SWOR)
* *fpc* is the proportion of the population that is not sampled
* *fpc* terms are generally ignored unless explicitly stated

* <u>Simple Random Sampling</u>

$$\text{var}(\bar{y}) = \left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right)s^2 / n = (1 - f)s^2 / n$$

where:
$n$ - sample size
$N$ - population size

<u>Stratified Random Sampling</u>

$$\text{var}(\bar{y}_{st}) = \sum_{h=1}^{H} (1 - f_h)W_h^2 s_h^2 / n_h$$

where: $f_h = n_h / N_h$

# Stratified Sampling

<u>Procedure</u>

* form strata
* independent selections in each stratum
* estimate for stratum h: $\bar{y}_h$

* overall estimate: $\bar{y}_{st} = \sum W_h \bar{y}_h$

  where $W_h = N_h / N$

* sampling variance: $\sum W_h^2 \text{Var}(\bar{y}_h)$

* estimated sample variance: $\sum W_h^2 \text{var}(\bar{y}_h)$

# Cluster Sampling

Many populations are widely distributed geographically. We cannot afford visits to $n$ units drawn randomly from the entire area.

Cluster sampling reduces the costs of data collection.
* Sample schools and children within them
* Sample blocks and households within them

Cluster sampling is also useful when the sampling frame lists clusters and not elements. In such a case, select clusters and list elements in selected clusters from which a sample of elements may be drawn:
* Frame of blocks; list households within selected blocks

# Cluster Sampling Examples (Complete Clusters)

For the sake of simplicity, we assume that each of the $A = 500$ blocks in the town has exactly $B = 24$ children. We select a SRS of $a = 10$ blocks and examine the immunization history of all children in the selected blocks. The sample consists of $n = aB = 240$ children.

We refer to the $A$ clusters as *primary sampling units*, or PSUs. For each of the $a = 10$ PSUs that are in the sample, we know exactly the proportion of children immunized. Suppose the following are those proportions:

$$y_{\alpha\beta} = \begin{matrix} 1 & \text{if immunized} \\ 0 & \text{if not} \end{matrix}$$

$$p_\alpha = \Sigma y_{\alpha\beta} / B\text{:}$$

$$\frac{9}{24}, \frac{11}{24}, \frac{13}{24}, \frac{15}{24}, \frac{16}{24}, \frac{17}{24}, \frac{18}{24}, \frac{20}{24}, \frac{20}{24}, \frac{21}{24}$$

# DESIGN EFFECT FOR A CLUSTER SAMPLE

* The design effect for the mean of a cluster sample depends on the cluster size ($B$) and the homogeneity of the elements within the clusters, measured by the intra-class correlation ($\rho$):

$$D^2(\bar{y}) = 1 + (B - 1)\rho.$$

* For estimates of means or proportions, the intra-class correlation may be estimated by:

$$\hat{\rho} = \frac{d^2(\bar{y}) - 1}{B - 1}.$$

* Thus

$$\hat{\rho} = \frac{3.091 - 1}{24 - 1} = 0.091.$$

43

<!-- page 44 -->

# INTRA-CLASS CORRELATION $\rho$

* $\rho$ is a property of the naturally occurring clusters and of the variable under study.
* Since elements in a cluster tend to be similar to one another, $\rho$ is virtually always positive. For human populations, a positive $\rho$ may be due to:
  - *Environment*: exposure to the same climate, hazardous waste, or infectious disease.
  - *Self selection*: wealthy households tend to reside in wealthy neighborhoods and poor households in poor neighborhoods.
  - *Interaction*: shared attitudes among neighbors.
* The magnitude of $\rho$ depends on:
  - The variable under study (e.g., newspaper read, age).
  - The nature of the clusters (e.g., households, city blocks, schools).
  - The size of the clusters (e.g., blocks, enumeration districts, counties).

* Sample $a$ blocks and $b$ children from each block. Blocks are termed primary sampling units (PSUs).

* Consider various combinations of $a$ and $b$, all with
  $n = ab = 240$. All these designs are *epsem*, with

P(\alpha\beta) = P(\alpha)P(\beta|\alpha) = \frac{\text{a}}{500} \times \frac{b}{24} = \frac{240}{500 \times 24} = \frac{1}{50}
