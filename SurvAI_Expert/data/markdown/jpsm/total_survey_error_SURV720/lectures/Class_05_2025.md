# Methods for Assessing Nonresponse Bias

1. Benchmarking (comparing *estimates*)
2. Comparing *characteristics* of respondents and non-respondents
3. Compare *responses* given by different *groups* of respondents (e.g., early vs late responders)
4. Compare $y$ values (methodological studies)

4

<!-- page 5 -->

# 1. Benchmarking

* Compare estimates obtained to estimates from other sources that are assumed to be more accurate
* Example: Keeter et al. (2000) compared estimates from “standard” 5-day survey (RR: 37%) to those from “rigorous” 8-week survey (RR: 61%)
  * Mean difference over 91 items around 2%
* Limitation: may confound NR error with other error sources

5

<!-- page 6 -->

# 2. Compare Characteristics of Responders and Nonresponders

* Data about nonrespondents can come from:
  - Information on sampling frame
  - Paradata such as interviewer observations
  - Responses to screener interviews
  - Responses to other survey waves
  - Administrative records

* Example: Groves and Couper (1998)
  - Response to six household surveys evaluated using matched 1990 Census data
  - Lower contact rates for multiunit structures, single person households, households in urban areas.
  - Cooperation rates lower (refusal rates higher) in middle-aged households, those who live alone.

# Relationship between response rates and nonresponse bias

* Higher response rate decreases the risk of nonresponse bias, other things being equal. On average.
* But *overall* relationship is generally weak
  - Groves and Peytcheva, 2008 : 959 estimates from 59 studies: correlation between resp. rates and NR bias $= 0.2$ ($\text{R}^2$ of $0.04$)
  - Brick and Tourangeau, 2017 : Study-level NR bias more correlated with the response rate than estimate-level bias ($\text{R}^2 = 0.23$)

# Reasons not to blindly pursue a high response rate

* Weak empirical link between nonresponse rates and estimate-level bias
* Consumes resources that could be used to reduce other errors
* Focus could be on increasing response rates of groups that are underrepresented, not on increasing the overall response rate
* Focus could be on other metrics (e.g., "R-indicators") that are more closely related to nonresponse bias

# Identification of NR Bias

* Groves et al. (2008) identifies two major types:
  - Survey level
  - Statistic level

23

<!-- page 24 -->

# A. Survey-level Indicators

* Variance of response rates on subgroups defined for all sample cases (respondents and nonrespondents)
  - As variance increases, presumed data quality decreases
* Goodness of fit statistics on propensity models
  - As model fit increases, presumed data quality decreases
* Variance of estimated response propensities (R-indicators)
  - As variance increases, presumed data quality decreases

24

<!-- page 25 -->

# Survey-level Indicators: R-indicator

* Basic procedure:
  * Fit a regression model with “response” as dependent variable and unit characteristics as independent variables
  * Estimate the probability of response ($\rho_i$) for each responding unit using its characteristics
* Most basic R-indicator is $1 - 2\text{ SD}(\rho)$
  * $\rho$: response propensities; SD: standard deviation

25

<!-- page 26 -->

# R-indicator...

* As R-indicator increases, presumed data quality increases
* Points to NR bias if response probabilities are variable. Implies strong association between nonresponse and auxiliary variables
  - Maximum $S(\rho) = .5$ and R-indicator $= 0$
* Points to negligible NR bias if response probabilities are equal. Implies weak association between nonresponse and auxiliary variables.
  - In this case $S(\rho) = 0$ and R-indicator $= 1$
* Estimate $\rho$. Dependent on the model (comparing R-indicators for different surveys?)

# B. Estimate-level Indicators

* Comparisons of respondents and nonrespondents on auxiliary variables
  - As differences increase, presumed data quality decreases
* Correlation between post-survey nonresponse adjustment weights and $y$ (from the respondent cases)
  - As correlation increases, presumed data quality decreases

# Estimate-level indicators…

* Level of uncertainty about values one would impute for current nonresponders on $y$ (“Fraction of Missing Information”)
  - Imputation models based on frame data and paradata
  - Missing data that cannot be recovered via nonresponse adjustments
  - As FMI decreases, presumed data quality increases

# Responsive (and Adaptive) Survey Design

'Responsive' and 'adaptive' survey design often used interchangeably to describe a whole group of strategies for managing data collection including:

- front loading potentially difficult cases
- tailoring data collection strategies to different subgroups
- prioritizing effort according to estimated response propensities
- imposing stop rules for ending data collection
- monitoring key survey estimates throughout the field period
- using two-phase or multiphase sampling for following up non-respondents
- calculating indicators of non-response bias (such as the R-indicator) other than response rates to monitor and guide fieldwork.

(Tourangeau et al. 2017)

40

<!-- page 41 -->

# Responsive Survey Design

* Efforts to monitor data quality indicators during a survey in order to change design features on the basis of those indicators.
* Often involves concentrating resources on ‘hard-to-interview’ cases and reducing effort on ‘easy’ cases in the sample
  * Potential design changes to target ‘hard’ cases: more contacts, larger incentive, costlier mode, assigning most successful interviewers, etc.

41

<!-- page 42 -->

# While ‘adaptive” and “responsive” often used interchangeably:

* Responsive: “Phases”
* Multiple protocols introduced in a phased design over the course of a study.
* Concept of “phase capacity”:
  – Additional data not contributing to further changes in the survey estimates
  – Can be cost-inefficient compared to starting another phase with a different and complementary protocol

* Adaptive: “Tailoring”
  - Some individuals within a phase can be assigned to a different treatment.
  - Different protocols can be applied to a subset of the sample, whether because they are more valuable for the survey estimates or because they are more likely to respond to the new protocol relative to the rest of the sample.
* Responsive and adaptive designs are complementary as adaptive designs in surveys are often implemented within phases of a responsive design.

*Peytchev, Pratt, Duprey, 2022*

# Responsive/Adaptive Designs

- Divide field period into phases and let initial phase inform data collection protocols in later phase. *Examples*:
  - Phase 1 survey shows that single-person households have lower response rates than other households. In Phase 2 special efforts are made to recruit single-person households.
  - Phase 1 data indicated that, by 14 call attempts, the estimates were unlikely to significantly change. In Phase 2 call attempts are capped at 14.
- Change survey protocols during data collection.

# Example of Case-prioritization
## High School Longitudinal Study of 2009

* Survey of parents; data collected initially by CATI and the Web
* Propensity models fit on six weeks’ data.
* Pursue lowest propensity quartile cases using CAPI.
* Results:
  - Boosted response rate in the lowest propensity quartile
  - Significant bias reduction for some variables

Case prioritization especially useful when it impacts final estimates (rather than go by response propensities alone).

Prioritizing Low Propensity Sample Members in a Survey: Implications for Nonresponse Bias, Rosen et al., Survey Practice, 2014.

# Example: Coffey et al. (2019) “*Dynamic* adaptive design”

* National Survey of College Graduates (2015)
* Goal: Improve ‘representativeness’ while monitoring the effect of interventions on response rates and survey costs.
* Split-sample experiment: traditional methods vs DAD
* y: unemployment, income, etc.; Z: degree, gender, disability status, etc.
* Compute, closely watch, and intervene based on R-indicators.
  * Cases classified as ‘enhanced effort’, ‘reduced effort’, or ‘standard effort’
* Improved quality without increasing cost or reducing response rate.

# Responsive/Adaptive Design

* Strengths:
  - Can address sample balance not just response rates
  - Cost reduction

* Limitations:
  - Harder to implement because different survey protocols used for different groups
  - Can result in smaller sample size
  - Need good data on Rs and NRs to inform
  - Often requires metrics that are diagnostic of nonresponse bias that can be monitored during the study
