# Weaknesses of the TSE Framework

1. Exclusions of key quality concepts
   - Notably the relevance to the user

2. Diagrammatic representation might give an impression of independence of error sources

3. Lack of routine measurements
   - error/quality profiles are useful but rare
   - empirical estimation of errors is hard to achieve (but can make use of ‘effect generalizability’.); large design burden.

<!-- page 13 -->

# Weaknesses of the TSE Framework...

4. Does not define acceptable error thresholds

5. Ineffective influence on professional standards
    - press releases by many survey organizations rarely mention anything beyond sampling errors (if that)

<!-- page 14 -->

# Strengths of the TSE Framework

1. Decomposition of errors
   - nomenclature for different components

2. Separation of phenomena affecting statistics in different ways
   - measurement vs. representation issues
   - variance vs. bias
   - provides way to evaluate alternative data collection procedures (trade-offs, resource allocations)

3. Conceptual foundation of the field of survey methodology
   * subfields defined by errors

# Cost-Error Constraints

* Surveys are conducted with constraints
* Three common types :
  * Set budget. Minimize errors within \$____.
  * Range of acceptable costs. Explore design options that produce different error levels within cost range.
  * Minimize cost for a given error level, e.g., Sampling variance $\le$ ___.

<!-- page 19 -->

# Traditional Cost Model

* Total cost = Fixed costs + Variable costs.
  - Fixed costs: costs of doing a survey regardless of its design.
  - Variable costs: costs that are a function design features.

19

<!-- page 20 -->

# Complicated Characteristics of Survey Costs

* Nonlinearity
  - Cost per case decreases as sample size increases
* Discontinuity
  - Need to add resources for every nth case
* Stochastic features
  - Variability in costs across cases
* Limited domains of applicability
  - Cost structure can change (cost model breaks down) in different organizations or with large design changes
  - Surveys with different lengths

20

<!-- page 21 -->

# Steps for Generating Cost-Error Model

1. Specify error model as function of design features.

2. Specify cost model in terms that are shared by the error model.

3. Combine equations into optimization problem that aims to minimize cost/error subject to an error/cost constraint.
   - e.g., determine the sample size and allocation to strata that minimizes error subject to a cost constraint

21

<!-- page 22 -->

# Limitations of Cost-Error Models

* Error models are for single estimates, for a single variable, and usually limited to sampling variance. And what about subpopulations?
* Error must be measurable and specified as a function of design features
  - In practice, this is more feasible for sampling error than other error sources
* Difficult to account for complicated characteristics of survey costs
* Major alternative designs have different budgets

# Incidentally, another reason not to trust AI blindly...

**Abstract Population:** "All young adults in the United States."

* This is an abstract population because there is no perfect, comprehensive list of every single young adult in the U.S. at any given moment. People are constantly aging into and out of the category, moving, or changing their contact information.
* You can't get a list of names and addresses for all of them, so you have to work with a sample.

**In contrast, a "concrete population" would be:**

* **Example 1:** "All students currently enrolled at a specific university." You could obtain a complete list of these students from the registrar's office.
* **Example 2:** "All members of the American Academy of Health Behavior." The organization would have a directory or database of all their members.

<!-- page 34 -->

You’ll see different terms used (clarify!):
* Inferential population (Want results from a sample survey to be generalized to this. Conceptual.)

* Target population (Set of elements to be studied. Time restrictions. Observable.)

* Frame population (Set of TP members that have a chance to be selected into the sample. ‘Operational’.)

* ‘Survey population’ (“Set of FP who might be Rs”. Accessible. Practical.)

Weishan calls this a “cascade relationship”

# Area Frames

* Collection of geographic units
* Area sampling process:
  - Define geographic unit (e.g., counties, metropolitan areas, zip code areas, sections from satellite image)
  - Randomly select areas
  - List units within those areas
  - Select subset of housing units
* Used primarily for in-person surveys

42

<!-- page 43 -->

# Address-Based Frames

* List of household addresses
  - Based on Postal Service’s Computerized Delivery Sequence (CDS) file, which provides nearly 100% coverage in many areas and is updated regularly
* ABS process:
  - Acquire address list
  - If in-person survey: select geographic areas
  - Select housing units from selected geographic areas
* Used for in-person interviews and surveys that start with a mail invitation, including “web-push” surveys

43

<!-- page 44 -->

# Characteristics of Area Frames and ABS

## Area Frame

* On-site enumeration by listers (?)
* Geographic location is known
* Potential coverage issues:
  - subject to listing errors
  - hard-to-see housing units

## ABS

* No listing required (less expensive)
* Geographic information not known (can be added through geocoding)
* Potential coverage issues:
  - newly constructed housing units
  - households' P.O. boxes
  - some units are businesses, not residences

<!-- page 45 -->

# RDD Frame

* Randomly-generated telephone numbers

* **R**andom **D**igit **D**ialing (RDD) process:
  * Define “banks” of 100 phone numbers with the same area code and next 5 digits (XXX-XXX-XX00 through XXX-XXX-XX99)
  * Identify banks with some minimum number of listed residential numbers (at least for landline RDD)
  * Randomly call phone numbers in these banks

# Register Frame

* Administrative list of units complied for some other purpose
  – UMD/UMICH alumni database; AAPOR member database; Census Business Register; IRS list of registered nonprofits; NASS list frame of agricultural producers; NCES list frame of private schools
  – Quality depends on the list and how it is maintained
    - Frequency of new units being added
    - Is contact information updated regularly?
    - Are duplicates removed?

# Coverage Bias

For a mean, error due to undercoverage can be decomposed into terms for the proportion of target population units that are <u>not covered (U)</u> **AND** on how different these units are from the units that <u>are covered (C)</u>

$$\bar{Y}_C - \bar{Y} = \frac{U}{N} (\bar{Y}_C - \bar{Y}_U)$$

# Measuring Undercoverage

* Most of the time the researcher has limited means of measuring coverage error
  – Both the noncoverage rate and difference between the noncovered and covered populations are unknown
* One strategy is to compare data from frame to external data (assumed to be better), e.g., Census data or data from another frame
