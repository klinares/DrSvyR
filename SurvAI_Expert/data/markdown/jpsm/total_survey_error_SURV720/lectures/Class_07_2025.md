# From Construct to Measurement

* Construct/Concept: Elements of information sought by researcher
  - Usually described by words, often abstract, permitting a definition
  - Examples: “job satisfaction”, “reading ability”, “religious attitudes”, “happiness”, “quality of life”, “crimes with victims”
* Measurement: Linking theoretical constructs to observable variables – ways to gather information about constructs
  - Examples: questions, observations, soil samples, blood pressure readings
  - Some constructs consist of just one attribute (e.g., age)
  - Other constructs are multi-dimensional with several attributes and need more than one Q to fulfill a particular objective (e.g., SES, attitudes)
  - Usually, numerous (competing) ways of how to measure a construct

* “Historically the most common definition of validity is that it refers to the extent to which a test or a set of operations measures what it is supposed to measure.”
  - Ghiselli et al. (1981)

* Construct validity refers to how well you translated or transformed a concept, idea, or behaviour that is a construct into a functioning and operating reality, the operationalization. – Taherdoost, 2016

* How useful/appropriate are the answers to my question in telling me about what I am trying to measure ?

<!-- page 10 -->

# Construct Validity in Surveys

* Goal of question development is to choose wording that can successfully operationalize construct

* Validity is about extent to which each question measures the corresponding construct
  * Is the measure appropriate (measuring what it’s intended to measure) and comprehensive (covering all aspects of the construct)?
  * Messick (1989): “even for purposes of applied decision making, reliance on criterion validity or content coverage is not enough. The meaning of the measure, and hence its construct validity, must always be pursued”.

* Mismatch between the information requested and the corresponding construct will produce invalid measures

# Assessing Validity

* **Qualitative** methods (e.g., cognitive interviewing; expert review): identify problems and reason for problems
  * Goal is not quantifying problems

* **Quantitative** methods (‘empirical validity’)
  * Validity of a measure is about correlation with the true score – sometimes have an observable true and sometimes not. If not:
  * identify the extent of problems and their statistical consequences
    * Correlation with another variable that on theoretical grounds should (or should not) be associated
    * Comparison between groups whose answers ought to differ if the answers are measuring the intended construct
    * Split-ballot type experiments

# Measurement Error

* Any deviations of the reported value from the “true” value
  - Can be systematic or random

* Has meaning at two levels
  - Respondent level
  - Estimate level

* Two ways of thinking about “truth”
  - Platonic true score
  - Classical true score

<!-- page 26 -->

# Respondent Level

* Focus is on i-th person's response deviations from her true value

* A single observation ($y_i$) is represented as the sum of two terms: a true value $\mu_i$ and an some measurement error, $\varepsilon_i$.

$$y_i = \mu_i + \varepsilon_i$$

* If follows that measurement error is the difference between their response and their true value

$$= y_i - \mu_i$$

26

<!-- page 27 -->

# Respondent Level…

* This can be further decomposed into conceptual replications of measurement, where answers (and response deviations) vary over trials.

* The expected observed value across trials ($\bar{y}_i$) is now represented as a true value $\mu_i$ and the expected value of error across trials for the respondent, $\bar{\varepsilon}_i$.

$$
\bar{y}_i = \mu_i + \bar{\varepsilon}_i
$$

* Respondent-level bias exists when $\varepsilon\text{’s}$ have a nonzero expected value (over conceptual replications of the questioning)

27

<!-- page 28 -->

# Respondent Level...

* Simple response variance is defined as the variance of responses over repeated trials
  – Will be zero if respondent provides consistent response under same conditions over time (‘reliability’)

* Consistency does not imply accuracy
  – Asking the same question at two points in time could yield the same answer, but the answer may be far from the true value

28

<!-- page 29 -->

# Estimate Level

* Focus is on the average error across respondents
* Respondent mean is represented as the true respondent mean and mean error across respondents (r):

$$
E(\bar{y}) = E \left( \frac{1}{r} \sum_{i=1}^{r} u_i + \frac{1}{r} \sum_{i=1}^{r} \varepsilon_i \right)
$$

  – Estimate is biased when $\bar{\varepsilon}$ has a nonzero expected value

29

<!-- page 30 -->

# Estimate Level...

* Random measurement error accounts for error variances across respondents
* If response deviations across respondents average to zero, does random measurement error have any statistical consequences?
  - For *variance of an estimate*, yes. The greater the meas. error variance, the larger the variance of $y$ will be, other things being equal.
  - For *point estimates*, it depends. Means and proportions are not affected, but correlation and regression coefficients will be attenuated (Biemer and Trewin, 1997)

# Platonic True Score

* Construct being measured is at least, in theory, verifiable based on tangible non-survey evidence
  - In other words, “truth” can be derived from external source
  - Applies to behaviors and some individual characteristics
  - Examples: someone’s vote in 2024 election, household size, BMI

* Survey researchers largely adhere to this view

36

<!-- page 37 -->

# Classical True Score

* Construct being measured has no existing real-world measure
  - Applies to psychological traits and attitudes
  - Applies to latent constructs measured using multi-item scales
  - Examples: extraversion, job satisfaction
* True score is conceptualized as expected value if something were remeasured over a very large number of trials
  - “psychometric perspective”

37

<!-- page 38 -->

# Classical True Score Cont.

* At respondent level, a single observation ($y_{it}$) is the sum of two terms: the mean of the response distribution for an individual $\mu_i^*$ and an error for the t-th response from it, $\varepsilon_{it}$.

$$
y_{it} = \mu_i^* + \varepsilon_{it}
$$

# Illustration: Measurement Trials...

* True score is the mean of response distribution across trials

  $(52+59+49+56)/4 = 54$

* Errors are the deviation from the true score:

  T1: $52-54 = -2$

  T2: $59-54 = 5$

  T3: $49-54 = -5$

  T4: $56-54 = 2$

Assumption is random error across trials (<u>this is not bias</u>).
