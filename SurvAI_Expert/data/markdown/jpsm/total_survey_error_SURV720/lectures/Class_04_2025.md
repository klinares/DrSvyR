# Hard-to-reach populations Network Sampling

* What do we mean by “hard-to-reach”?
* Multiplicity
  * Respondents report about events among those in their personal networks (i.e., acquaintances, friends, extended family)
* Snowball sampling (non-probability)
  * Initial recruits in convenience sample refer to other members in the population.

# Respondent-driven sampling (RDS)

* Effort to convert snowball sampling into a probability sampling method
* Recruit ‘seeds’ who then recruit others in their network

Source: Liu, Li, Ha, and Li (2012)

# Some conditions for RDS

* Must be possible to trace who recruited whom
* Should collect information concerning the size of each person’s network;
* Must have a maximum number of possible recruitments for a person
* Recruiter and recruit must have known each other prior to recruitment, each being a member of the other’s social network.
* But not fully straightforward…

# Nonresponse Error

* Arises when some sampled units do not respond
  - <u>Unit</u> nonresponse is the failure to obtain survey measures on a sample unit (our focus).

  - <u>Item</u> nonresponse is the failure to obtain an answer to a given item.

# Response Rates

* Proportion of estimated eligible sample who completed the survey

* Widely-used metric of survey participation:
  - Fairly simple to compute
  - Computation can be relatively standardized
  - Single number for the entire survey

<!-- page 19 -->

# AAPOR Dispositions for Response Rate Calculations

I = number of completed cases

P = number of partial cases

NC = number of noncontacted sample units known to be eligible

R = number of eligible refused cases

O = number of eligible sample units not responding for reasons other than refusal

U = number of sample units of unknown eligibility, not completed

e = estimated proportion of sample units of unknown eligibility that are eligible

19

<!-- page 20 -->

# AAPOR Response Rates

* 
$$
\text{RR1} = \frac{I}{(I + P) + (NC + R + O) + U}
$$

$$
\text{RR2} = \frac{I + P}{(I + P) + (NC + R + O) + U}
$$

$$
\text{RR3} = \frac{I}{(I + P) + (NC + R + O) + eU}
$$

$$
\text{RR4} = \frac{I + P}{(I + P) + (NC + R + O) + eU}
$$

# 1. Social Exchange Theory

(Homans 1961; developed for surveys by Dillman 1978)

* People respond if perceived benefits of responding outweigh costs of responding.

* Example benefits: having voice be heard; monetary incentive. Example costs: time, effort, loss of privacy.

# 2. Social integration (isolation) theory (Goyder 1987)

* Individuals who are more integrated in society will be more likely to respond to a survey.

  - Groves and Couper (1998): find lower contact rates and cooperation rates for those who live alone

## (Groves et al. 2000)

# Limitation of Past Theories

* Focus on cooperation; do not address contact process.
* Assume people make rational choices based on features of the study; do not address the use of quick judgments/rules. For example:
  * Throw out all mail, unless it’s clearly a bill.
  * Hang up/don’t answer telephone, unless it’s a personal call from someone you know.

# Measuring Nonresponse Error

# Nonresponse Error

* Nonresponse bias in a respondent mean can be expressed as:

$$
\text{Bias}(\bar{y}_r) = \bar{Y}_r - \bar{Y}
$$

* $\bar{Y}$ is the mean of the full <u>target population</u>
* $\bar{Y}_r$ is the mean of respondents in the <u>target population</u>

..or frame population ?

49

<!-- page 49 -->

# Nonresponse Bias Continued

* Nonresponse bias in a respondent mean can also be expressed as:
$$
\text{Bias}(\bar{y}_r) = \frac{M}{N}(\bar{Y}_r - \bar{Y}_m)
$$

* Bias depends on the nonresponse rate and the difference between respondent and nonrespondent means in the target population

# Methods for Assessing Nonresponse Bias

# 1. Benchmarking

* Compare estimates obtained to estimates from other sources that are assumed to be more accurate
* Example: Keeter et al. (2000) compared estimates from “standard” 5-day survey (RR: 37%) to those from “rigorous” 8-week survey (RR: 61%)
  * Mean difference over 91 items around 2%
* Limitation: may confound NR error with other error sources

59

<!-- page 58 -->

# 2. Compare Characteristics of Responders and Nonresponders

* Data about nonrespondents can come from:
  - Information on sampling frame
  - Paradata such as interviewer observations
  - Responses to screener interviews
  - Responses to other survey waves
  - Administrative records

* Example: Groves and Couper (1998)
  - Response to six household surveys evaluated using matched 1990 Census data
  - Lower contact rates for multiunit structures, single person households, households in urban areas.
  - Cooperation rates lower (refusal rates higher) in middle-aged households, those who live alone.

# 3. Compare Responses Given by Different Groups of Respondents

* Early vs. late responders
  - May be informative about the direction of response bias
* Nonresponse follow-up studies
  - Goal: to obtain responses from a random sample of the nonrespondents
* Those who express willingness to participate in a future survey vs. those who do not
