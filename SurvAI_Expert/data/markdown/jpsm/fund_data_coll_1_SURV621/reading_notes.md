**Krosnick, J. A., and S. Presser. 2010.**

Chapter 9: Question and Questionnaire Design

Key points from the chapter:

Questionnaire design is crucial for survey success. The questionnaire scripts the conversation between researchers and respondents, and its quality directly affects the accuracy of survey results.
Best practices for questionnaire design stem from both conventional wisdom and methodological research.
Open versus closed questions have different strengths and weaknesses for measuring different types of data (nominal categories, numeric quantities, factual knowledge).
Rating scales should be carefully designed to avoid biases and ensure accurate measurement. Factors to consider include the number of scale points, labeling, and response option order.
Response options should be exhaustive and mutually exclusive.
Question order can influence responses. Sensitive topics should be placed at the end, and questions on the same topic should be grouped together.
Testing and evaluating questionnaires is essential for identifying potential problems and improving the design.

Specific recommendations:

Use simple, clear language and avoid ambiguity.
Avoid leading or loaded questions.
Ask about one thing at a time.
Consider the cognitive burden on respondents when designing questions.
Pretest questionnaires to identify potential problems.

Overall, the chapter emphasizes the importance of careful questionnaire design to ensure accurate and reliable survey data.

Tourangeau, R. and T. Yan. 2007. “Sensitive questions in surveys,”

This article is about sensitive questions in surveys and how they can lead to inaccurate data.

Here are the key points:

Sensitive questions are those that make people uncomfortable or embarrassed to answer truthfully. This can be because the questions are intrusive, there is a risk of disclosure of personal information, or the questions ask about socially undesirable behaviors.

Sensitive questions can lead to three main problems:

Lower response rates: People may be less likely to participate in a survey if they know it will ask sensitive questions.

More item nonresponse: Even if people participate in a survey, they may skip over sensitive questions.
Misreporting: People may lie about their answers to sensitive questions, especially if they are worried about being judged or punished.

The article also discusses how researchers can try to reduce these problems by:

Wording questions carefully to make them less intrusive.
Assuring respondents that their answers will be confidential.
Using indirect questioning techniques.

Overall, the article highlights the importance of being aware of the potential problems caused by sensitive questions and taking steps to mitigate them.

### Couper 2011 The Future of modes of data collection

The study examines how different methods of data collection (modes) can influence the results of surveys. There are two main approaches to address this issue:

Prevention: This approach attempts to minimize the differences between modes by designing questionnaires that can be delivered effectively in any format.
Correction: This approach acknowledges that measurement errors will occur due to different modes, and then tries to statistically adjust the data to account for these errors.

The best approach may depend on the specific modes being used. For example, web surveys and face-to-face surveys are more versatile and can be adapted to minimize mode effects. On the other hand, traditional phone surveys are more limited.

The study also highlights the importance of reporting on the data collection methods used in a survey. This includes details about the specific modes used, the response rates for each mode, and any procedures used to adjust for mode effects.

Finally, the study discusses the future of data collection modes. New technologies are constantly emerging, and survey researchers need to find ways to adapt to these changes. However, it is important to balance innovation with the need to maintain comparability with past studies.

**Couper, M. 2000. “Web surveys: A review of issues and approaches,”**

This study examines the various approaches to conducting web surveys and assesses their strengths and weaknesses. There are two main categories:

Non-probability surveys: These rely on volunteers who opt-in to participate. They are quick and inexpensive to set up, but the results may not be representative of the general population because whoever chooses to participate may be biased.

Probability-based surveys: These involve recruiting participants through random sampling methods, similar to traditional phone surveys. This ensures the results can be applied to a broader population. However, they are more expensive and time-consuming to conduct.

The study details eight different types of web surveys:

* Opt-in panels: People volunteer to join a panel and receive invitations to surveys. (Non-probability)
* Web intercept surveys: Target visitors to a specific website and invite them to participate in a survey. (Non-probability)
* List-based samples: Target a specific population with known email addresses, such as students at a university. (Limited probability)
* Mixed-mode designs: Offer the survey via web along with other methods like phone or mail. (Can be probability or non-probability)
* Pre-recruited panels: Use probability methods like phone surveys to recruit participants for a web panel. (Probability)
* Probability samples of the full population: Provide equipment and internet access to participants in exchange for their participation in web surveys. (Probability)

The study concludes that web surveys offer a valuable tool for data collection, but emphasizes the importance of understanding the limitations of each approach. Researchers need to consider factors like coverage (who can access the survey), non-response rates (how many people don't complete the survey), and potential biases to ensure the results are reliable.

**AAPOR. 2010. “Chapter 3: An overview of online panels” and “Chapter 4: Errors**

**of nonobservation in online panel surveys”**

This study examines the issue of coverage error and nonresponse bias in online surveys that use volunteer panels. Here are the key points:

* Traditional surveys rely on probability sampling, where everyone in the target population has a chance of being selected. Online panels, however, are volunteer-based, meaning people sign up to participate.

* Because online panels don't use probability sampling, there's no way to know if the panel represents the entire target population (coverage error). For example, people without internet access or those who aren't interested in participating in surveys are not included.

* Online panels also suffer from nonresponse bias. This happens when people who choose to participate in the survey are systematically different from those who don't. For instance, people with more interest in a particular topic might be more likely to respond to a survey about it.

* The study explores different stages where nonresponse can occur in online panels:

  * Recruitment: People who are more likely to see and respond to recruitment messages might differ from the general population.

  * Joining and profiling: Some people who start signing up might not complete the process.

  * Specific study stage: People might refuse to participate in a particular survey for various reasons.

  * Panel maintenance: Members may drop out of the panel over time.

  * The study concludes that it's difficult to assess the exact impact of coverage error and nonresponse bias in online panels because there's no clear sampling frame or way to compare respondents to the target population.

  * Low response rates can be a sign of potential bias, but they don't necessarily mean the results are inaccurate. Some studies haven't found a strong link between response rates and bias in online panels.

Overall, the study highlights the limitations of online panels due to coverage error and nonresponse bias. It emphasizes the importance of considering these issues when interpreting results from online surveys.

**Tourangeau, R. Conrad, F., & Couper M. (2013). Chapter 4: Introduction to**

**measurement and design in web surveys**

This chapter of "The Science of Web Surveys" discusses design considerations for web-based surveys. Here are the key takeaways:

* Web surveys offer more design options than traditional paper surveys. This flexibility can be both an advantage and a disadvantage.

<!-- page 6 -->

* Research on how design choices affect responses is limited. There's a need for more studies to understand the impact of design on data accuracy and validity.
* Many web survey studies use college students or non-probability online panels. These participants might be more accustomed to web surveys and their findings may not generalize to the broader population.
* Careful design can improve data quality and reduce survey drop-out rates. However, there's no one-size-fits-all approach for web survey design.

The chapter focuses on specific design elements including:

* Question presentation: How answer choices are arranged (vertically/horizontally), where the answer selection tool is placed (left/right of the label) and how many columns are used to display options can all influence responses.
* Navigation buttons: The prominence of "Previous" and "Next" buttons can affect how quickly respondents complete the survey and whether they accidentally go back and change answers.
* Response formats: Web surveys offer various ways for respondents to provide answers, including radio buttons, checkboxes, drop-down menus, and text fields. The best format depends on the type of question being asked.
* Grid/Matrix questions: Grouping related questions into a grid format can save respondents time but might also lead to more incomplete data or measurement errors.

If you're designing a web survey, keep these considerations in mind to create a user-friendly and informative experience for participants.

Mobile survey apps can provide more tailored experiences and advanced features, but they require installation and may be less accessible to users with limited technical skills.
* Challenges and Future Directions: The field of mobile web surveys is still evolving, and there are challenges related to device diversity, respondent behavior, and data quality. Future research should focus on understanding the impact of different devices and interfaces on response quality, as well as developing innovative approaches to engage mobile respondents.

Overall, the study emphasizes the importance of adapting web survey design and implementation to the growing use of mobile devices. By understanding the unique characteristics of mobile devices and optimizing the survey experience, researchers can improve data quality and ensure the successful conduct of web surveys in the mobile era.

**Tourangeau, R. and T. W. Smith. 1996. "Asking sensitive questions: The impact of data collection mode, question format, and question context."**

The study by Tourangeau and Smith (1996) compares three methods of collecting survey data on sensitive topics like sexual behaviors. The research design involved:

1. **Participants:** 339 adults aged 18-45 from Cook County, Illinois.

2. **Methods:** Three data collection modes were compared:

   * **Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI):** Interviewer-administered using a computer.

   * **Computer-Assisted Self-Administered Interviewing (CASI):** Respondents use a computer without an interviewer.

   * **Audio Computer-Assisted Self-Administered Interviewing (ACASI):** Respondents use a computer with audio assistance for questions.

3. **Question Formats:** Open-ended vs. closed-ended questions about the number of sex partners.

4. **Context Variations:** Different contexts in which the questions were embedded.

**Key Findings:**

* **Response Rates:** No significant difference in response rates across the three modes.

* **Reporting of Sensitive Behaviors:** Self-administration methods (CASI and ACASI) led to more honest reporting of sensitive behaviors compared to CAPI.

* **Gender Disparity:** Self-administration reduced the disparity in reporting the number of sex partners between men and women.

* **Illicit Drug Use:** ACASI increased the proportion of respondents admitting to illicit drug use.

* **Question Format Impact:** Closed questions with low-end emphasis reported fewer sex partners compared to high-end emphasis

**Steiger, D. M, and B. Conroy. 2008. "IVR: interactive voice response," in de E.D.**

**Leeuw, J.J. Hox, D.A. Dillman (eds.)**

The chapter "The Use of Voice in IVR Research" discusses the role of voice in Interactive Voice Response (IVR) surveys. It explores how factors like gender, personalization, and the use of human or synthetic voices can influence respondent behavior and data quality.

Key findings and recommendations:

* Gender of the voice: While early studies hypothesized that the gender of the voice could influence responses, later research found no significant impact on reporting levels or response rates.

* Personalization: Personalizing question wording, such as using "Now I will ask you..." instead of "Next, please answer...", can lead to fewer embarrassing admissions.

* Human vs. synthetic voices: Studies have shown no significant difference in break-off rates or reporting of sensitive information between human and synthetic voices, even when respondents can distinguish between them.

* Questionnaire design: IVR questionnaires should be concise, clear, and straightforward, avoiding complex sentence structures, parentheses, and abbreviations.

* Respondent bias: Respondents may experience difficulties with IVR technology, especially if they are unfamiliar with it. To minimize errors, practice items can be included, and sufficient time should be allowed for responses.

* Mode effects: IVR can lead to different response patterns compared to other modes like CATI or paper-and-pencil surveys. Sensitive topics may be reported more honestly in self-administered modes like IVR.

* Nonresponse: IVR can have higher nonresponse rates compared to CATI, particularly due to break-offs during the transfer process or during the survey itself. Strategies to minimize break-offs include engaging respondents early on, keeping the survey concise, and providing clear instructions.

* Coverage error: The availability of touch-tone phones and the cost of international calls are important considerations for IVR surveys.

Overall, the chapter provides valuable insights into the strengths and limitations of IVR as a data collection method. By carefully considering the factors discussed, researchers can design effective IVR surveys that minimize bias and maximize data quality.

**De Leeuw, E.D. 2018. “Mixed mode: Past, present and future.”**

This article discusses mixed-mode surveys, a survey design that uses multiple data collection methods within a single study. The author, Edith D. de Leeuw, focuses on the historical development, current applications, and future directions of mixed-mode surveys.

**Research Design**

* The article reviews the history of mixed-mode surveys, dating back to the 1980s with the use of mail, phone, and face-to-face interviews.
* It categorizes mixed-mode surveys into two main types:
  * Using multiple modes of contact (e.g., advance letters before phone surveys)
  * Using multiple modes of data collection (e.g., offering phone, online, or mail survey options)

**Findings**

* Mixed-mode surveys offer several advantages, including:
  * Improved coverage by reaching a wider range of respondents who prefer different methods.
  * Increased response rates by offering flexible options.
  * Reduced costs compared to solely relying on expensive methods like face-to-face interviews.
  * Potential for better measurement by using different modes for sensitive questions (e.g., self-administered options for privacy).
* Challenges of mixed-mode surveys include:
  * Increased logistical complexity in managing different data collection methods.
  * Potential for mode effects, where data collected through different modes might not be directly comparable due to question presentation or respondent behavior.
* The article explores techniques for addressing mode effects, such as statistical adjustments and careful question design.
* The future of mixed-mode surveys involves adapting to new technologies like smartphones and tablets, while considering the changing social customs of communication.
* Combining mixed-mode surveys with big data (e.g., social media or administrative records) offers opportunities for richer data collection but requires careful consideration of data quality and ethical issues.

Overall, the article emphasizes the growing importance of mixed-mode surveys in survey research while acknowledging the need for ongoing development to address new technologies and social contexts.

**Biemer, Paul P., and Lars E. Lyberg. 2003. “Chapter 5: Errors due to Interviewers and Interviewing.” Introduction to survey quality. John Wiley & Sons: Hoboken, NJ, 149-187**

This chapter discusses different methods for collecting survey data. The best method depends on the goals of the survey, the characteristics of the mode, and the resources available. Here's the key question to consider when choosing a data collection mode:

What are the relative advantages and disadvantages of each mode for my specific survey?\*

The chapter explores three main modes of data collection:

* Face-to-Face Interviewing: This is the most flexible mode but also the most expensive. It allows for long, complex interviews and reduces social desirability bias (where respondents give answers they think are favorable). However, interviewers can introduce bias and completing interviews can be time-consuming.
* Telephone Interviewing: This is less expensive than face-to-face interviews but has limitations. Interviews must be shorter and visual aids cannot be used. Telephone coverage is also a concern as some people may not have phones or use features that block calls.
* Mail Surveys: This is the cheapest mode but can have lower response rates. Mail surveys allow for thoughtful answers and avoid interviewer bias, but there's no control over who completes the questionnaire and complex designs are difficult.

The chapter also discusses computer-assisted versions of face-to-face (CAPI) and telephone (CATI) interviewing, which offer advantages like reducing skip errors and allowing for more complex questionnaires.

The article argues that reducing the sample size of the Current Population Survey (CPS) would have significant negative consequences. While it might save some money, it would also degrade the quality of labor market data, making it harder for policymakers to make informed decisions.

Reducing the sample size would make it more difficult to detect changes in labor market outcomes, particularly for smaller population groups and geographic areas. This could lead to delayed or ineffective policy responses.

The article also highlights that the CPS is the only reliable source of information for certain labor market measures, such as labor force participation and unemployment duration. These measures are essential for understanding the health of the labor market and informing economic policy.

In conclusion, while reducing the CPS sample size may save some money, it would come at a significant cost to the quality of labor market data. This could have negative consequences for policymakers and the economy as a whole.
