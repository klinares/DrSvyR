# Interviews vs. Self-administration

* Interviewers can improve quality
  * Recruit sample members who might not otherwise participate
  * Observe surroundings (in-person interviews) to provide context for responses
  * Motivate *Rs*, clarify *Qs* and response task
  * Collect biomarker (e.g., blood) and physical (e.g., grip strength) data in person
* Interviewers can introduce error when
  * responses elicited by $> 1$ *Iwer* are differently distributed (creating variance)\*
  * their “observable” attributes, e.g., race, gender, age, facial expressions, are relevant to *Q* topic and respondent’s value (creating bias)
* Interviewers are a human resource
  * Expensive: Single greatest budget line in surveys with interviewers
  * Cannot work around the clock

\*Need random assignment of *Rs* to *Iwers* *to measure rho (rate of homogeneity) which is a variable specific population parameter for a given design*

# Listing, Undercoverage and Coverage Bias

* Eckman & Kreuter (2013)
  * Examined two cycles of listing in NSFG
  * 86% of housing units listed first time were listed second time, i.e., 13% undercoverage
    * undercoverage attributed to multiunit status, rural areas, and map difficulties
  * Coverage bias: Units not covered in second listing (but listed in first) different than units covered in second listing on several attributes: birth control use, pregnancies, education and income
    * Survey data available for undercovered units from first listing make it possible to determine bias
* Authors argue undercoverage from listing should concern data users

# Kish (1949) Roster Method

1. List all members of housing unit/household, then check eligibility of members

2. Number the eligible males from oldest to youngest then eligible females from oldest to youngest

3. Use selection table (“cover sheet”) to select respondent
    * Assures all eligible *Rs* in HH have equal chance of selection
    * 8 selection tables randomly allocated to each household

# Kish Roster Method: Pros and Cons

* Advantage
  * Most precise selection method: each HH member has equal (and known) probability of selection
  * Can be automated
  * Can be extended to oversample groups of interest (e.g., teenagers, males, etc.)
* Disadvantages
  * Requires gender and age of all eligible HH members
  * Intrusive, time consuming
  * Subject to interviewer error

# Birthday Methods

* Last birthday (Oldendick, Bishop and Tuchfarber, 1988):

  *May I speak to the person in the household who is 18 years of age or older and whose birthday was most recent?*

* Next Birthday (Salmon and Nichols, 1983):
  * Interviewer asks to speak to the adult member of the household who has the next birthday

* Birthday methods not probability method – selection probabilities unknown – but introduces quasi-randomness

29

<!-- page 30 -->

# Birthday Selection Methods: Advantages

* Advantages
  * Relatively easy to administer
  * Equal (but unknown) probability of selection within household
  * Less intrusive, faster, and lower effort for HH member than Kish method
  * Generates sample that ~random

# Last and Next Birthday Selection Methods: Disadvantages

* Exact chance of selection is unknown – “quasi-probability method” (Gaziano, 2005) because don’t know how many eligible sample members in HH
* Subject to reporting error; difficult to verify
* Biemer & Lyberg (2003) recommend <u>against</u> use in rigorous, scientific studies
    * But many serious studies use one of the birthday methods

# Hybrid R-Selection Method

* Rizzo, Brick & Park (2004) propose method that is minimally invasive, reduces recruitment effort, and usually explicit about $p(\text{selection})$
* Ask $R$ in screener how many adults in household
  * If $\text{N} = 1$, screener $R$ selected; end of process
  * If $\text{N} > 1$, randomly sample with $p(\text{selection}) = 1/\text{N}$
    * If $\text{N} = 2$ and screener $R$ not selected, then other selected
    * If $\text{N} > 2$ and screener $R$ not selected, then use Last Birthday method (or Kish when birthdays not known) to select from remaining adults
* Rizzo et al. tested in HINTS (RDD survey of where American adults acquire health info)
  * Male and female proportions close to what expect from CPS

# Choosing Among Respondent Selection Methods

* Response errors affect all methods
    * Kish method requires enumeration of HH which may entail measurement error
    * Birthday methods reveal purpose of questioning; easy to manipulate if, e.g., HH member does not want to participate
* Full listings of households require explaining the rationale of *R*-selection process to household member
    * May lower overall response rates
* Different organizational cultures regarding preferences for different methods; different researcher preferences

# Proxy Respondents

* Is it okay to collect data about selected *R* from someone other than selected *R*, *i.e.*, a proxy *R*?
* Several advantages
  * Less time and, therefore expense, to use proxy reports (e.g., CPS reduces cost by about 12%)
  * Using proxy *R*s results in higher response rates
* General belief that self-reports are better than proxy reports
  * Proxies’ *response process* thought to rely more on general knowledge than actual events (Blair, Menon and Bickart, 1991; Sudman et al., 1994) compared to self-reporters

# Self/Proxy Differences as Actor/Observer Differences

* Schwarz and Wellens (1997) tested this idea in Self/Proxy context:
  * Self-reporters’ answers based on memory for specific autobiographical events
  * Proxy *Rs’* reports draw more on general knowledge (dispositions) of “the other,” i.e., the intended/selected *R*
  * Intended/selected *Rs’* answers (which are about themselves) should reflect more *inconsistencies* in behavior – departures from usual routine – than in proxy reports

# Standardization Debate: Goals and Assumptions of Standardized Interviewing

* Promote *Iwers* being interchangeable
  * Should not matter who asks the *Q*s
* Minimize interviewer-related error (variance)
  * Assuming *Rs* randomly assigned to *Iwers* and standardized administration, distribution of answers should not vary by *Iwer*
* Promote comparability of answers
  * if *Q* (stimulus) is identical for all *R*s, different answers from different *R*s cannot be due to *Q* wording
  * differences assumed to be due to actual differences between *R*s

41

<!-- page 42 -->

# Standardized Interviewing

* *Iwer* reads *Q*s exactly as worded in questionnaire
* If *R*'s utterance is anything other than acceptable response, administer neutral (or non-directive) probe, *e.g.*, “Let me repeat the question”
  * *Iwer* chooses whether/how to probe so has some discretion, i.e., not fully standardized
  * Variation in probe wording involves non-subtantive info, i.e., *Q* wording always the same
* Ask *Q*s in the order they appear in questionnaire
  * Otherwise, context of *Q* differs changing stimulus; makes answers hard to compare
* Ask every *Q* even if *R* has previously volunteered relevant info
  * *R* might answer *Q* differently than what the previously volunteered info implies
* Be professional: avoid reactions that *R* might interpret as evaluative

# Examples of Neutral (Non-directive) Probes

* After *R* answers *Q*:
  * “Is that ‘yes’ or ‘no’?”
  * “Could you be a little bit more specific?”
  * “Which comes closest to the way you feel?”

* After *R* requests Clarification:
  * “Let me reread the question”
  * “I’m sorry, but I don’t have that information”
  * “Whatever it means to you.”

# Criticism of Standardized Wording

* The chief criticism is that standardized <u>wording</u> prevents *conversational grounding* (e.g., Clark, 1996; Suchman & Jordan, 1990)
  * Grounding: Speaker and listener talk back and forth until they agree they understand each other well enough for purposes of current task
  * In survey interviews, establishing grounding can require different wording for different *Rs*, i.e. wording not strictly standardized

* Goal should be to produce uniform interpretation across *Rs* so *Iwer* should aim to standardize <u>meaning</u> rather than <u>wording</u>
  * While standardized wording may promote <u>reliable</u> responses (e.g., same answers from same *Rs* on different occasions), not necessarily <u>valid</u> answers
  * assuring that *Rs* understand *Q* in the same way – which grounding helps achieve – should promote valid (i.e., accurate) responses.

# When is Standardized Wording Sufficient?

* When all *Rs* interpret question as intended
  * Everyone would probably agree that if a married couple with two young children live in a house, and no one else ever sleeps there, then four people live in the house
* But what about situations in which *Rs* do not know what to include or exclude?
  * What if one child is away at college?
  * What if a nanny sleeps in the house most nights, or a border is temporarily living in the house?

# Standardized vs. Conversational Interviews

* Schober & Conrad (1997) asked *Rs* (in lab) 12 *Qs* from US gov. surveys
* *Rs* answered on basis fictional scenarios that allowed researchers to measure response accuracy
  * ½ scenarios, *Q* ambiguous w/o a definition: *complicated mappings*
    * e.g., answer *Q* about furniture purchases on basis receipt for buying floor lamp
  * other ½, *Q* clear (unambiguous) w/o definition: *straightforward mapping*
    * e.g., receipt for purchase of end table
* Two different interviewing techniques:
  * Standardized Interviewing (SI) where *Iwer* cannot deviate from script
  * Conversational Interviewing (CI)*:
    1) encouraged *R* to ask questions if did not understand something
    2) *Iwer* able to clarify word meanings, e.g., whether someone who babysits for multiple families has 1 or $>1$ job, <u>even if changed wording for different *Rs*</u>

* Called “Flexible Interviewing” in 1997 paper; came to be called “Conversational Interviewing” in later papers

# How Often Does Conversational Interviewing Help?

* Assessed through difference in response accuracy\* between CI and SI in a national sample (Conrad & Schober, 2000)
  * Response quality measured as <u>change</u> between initial standardized and either subsequent standardized or conversational interview
* Logic:
  * *R*'s interpretation in SI (i.e., no clarification available) may be changed (corrected) by clarification in subsequent CI leading *R* change answer in second (conversational) interview
  * If initial interpretation (misconception) persists in subsequent SI, less likely to change answer

\*indirect measure

# Standardization Debate: Summary

- *Conversational interviews* can substantially improve response accuracy compared to *standardized interviews*
  - when scope of questions is ambiguous, e.g., what should be included and what should be excluded
  - because conversational interviewers can ground the meaning of the terms and phrases in *Q*
- Grounding takes time so duration of conversational interviews greater than strictly standardized interviews
- Does Standardized Interviewing actually reduce interviewer variance?
