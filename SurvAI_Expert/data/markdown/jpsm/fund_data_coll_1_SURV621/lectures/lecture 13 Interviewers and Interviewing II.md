# Personal vs. Formal Interviewing

* Two approaches to interviewing explored by Dijkstra (1987): “Formal” and “Personal” interviewing styles
  * Formal: business-like, no small talk
  * Personal: connect with *R* on an individual basis (e.g., “Good for you” or “Me too”)
  * Note: $\neq$ conversational vs. standardized interviewing distinction
* Both approaches require learning basics of interviewing, e.g., reading questions as worded, probing, as well as specifics of style
  * Formal style includes “unlearning” person-oriented behavior

# Personal vs. Formal Interviewing (2)

* Dijkstra randomized *Iwers* to formal or personal interviews and asked:
  * will Personal style <u>motivate</u> *Rs* to answer as accurately as possible or will it lead them to try to please *Iwer*, i.e., to <u>ingratiate</u> themselves
* and measured impact on accuracy and social desirability
* Accuracy:
  * asked *Rs* draw map of their hometown
  * coded for proportional correctness and inclusion of key landmarks, etc.
* Social Desirability:
  * Administered shortened version of Marlowe-Crowne impression management scale
  * Higher scores indicate tendency toward more socially desirable responses

# Rapport and Disclosure

* Possible increased rapport will inhibit disclosure of sensitive info because *R* wishes to maintain *Iwer's* favorable impression of them
* Sun et al. (2021) manipulated question sensitivity (low, moderate, high) and measured rapport between *Iwer* and *R*
  * Sensitivity of *Q*s independently rated
  * Collected *R*s' ratings of rapport with *Iwer* immediately after interview
  * Found more disclosure when *R*s rated rapport high suggesting rapport promotes candor more than it promotes impression management
  * Mechanism seems to be reduced social distance between *Iwer* and *R* created by increased rapport

# Rapport and Acceptable Responses: *Iwer*’s Nonverbal behavior

* Welles, Sun & Miller (2022) examined relationship between “adequate responses”\* and non-verbal behavior of 4 NSFG\*\* *Iwers* known to affect/reflect rapport: smiling, nodding, direct gaze
* Adequate responses more likely when *Iwer* engaged in the three non-verbal behaviors
  * Direction of causality unclear but implication is rapport => better responses
* Relationship unaffected by degree of standardization in interaction
  * Suggests may be possible for *Iwers* to build relationship with *R* using nonverbal behaviors without deviating from standardized techniques

\* responses that matched one of the expected response categories and required no additional probing
\*\*National Survey of Family Growth

# Summary: Personal vs. Formal Interviewing; Effects of Rapport on Disclosure and Acceptable Responses

* Personal style motivates respondents to give accurate and candid responses relative to formal style
  * Minor increase in ingratiation
* Seems to work by increasing rapport between *I* and *R*
  * Note: Personal $\neq$ Conversational Interviewing
* Increased rapport seems to increase disclosure of moderately and extremely sensitive behavior, presumably by reducing social distance
* *Iwer* non-verbal behaviors associated with increased rapport (smiling, nodding, direct gaze) are more common when responses acceptable

# Motivating and “Training” Respondents

* Cannell, Miller & Oxenberg (1987) developed an interlocking set of techniques to positively affect how *Rs* approach their task
* Experimental treatment bundles together three techniques intended to improve quality of responses, compared to none of these in control group

1. *Commitment*
    * Verbal agreement by respondent to give accurate and complete information
2. *Instructions*
    * Statements read by *I* for specific *Qs*: “For the next few questions, you may need to take time to think carefully before you answer to be the most accurate.”
3. *Feedback*
    * Both <u>positive</u> (“I see, this is the kind of exact answer we need.”) and <u>negative</u> (“You answered that quickly. Are there any days you might have overlooked?”)

# Commitment in Web Surveys

* *Rs* can be asked by an *Iwer* to commit to conscientious responding but can also be asked in web questionnaire
* Conrad et al. (2017): online *Rs* asked to commit; less likely to speed and (in one experiment) to straightline
* Cibelli-Hibben et al., (2020): online *Rs* asked to commit; less likely to skip items, more likely to check records, responded more slowly, reported income more accurately than control

# Interviewer Effects

* Interviewers add value to the survey enterprise
  * e.g., increase response rates, explain *Q* meaning to *R*, conduct within-HH sampling
* But can also **change** [introduce] error – *interviewer effects*
* 2 main kinds of interviewer effects (coverage error, nonresponse error, sampling error and **measurement error**), due to:
  * *Iwer* behavior (administering questionnaire); affects *Iwer*-related **<u>variance</u>**; measured for individual questions, i.e., not entire q’aire
  * *Iwer* fixed attributes, e.g., race, gender, age; associated with directional error, i.e., **<u>bias</u>**, for questions related to attribute, e.g., gender of interviewer effects for *Q*s on gender-relevant topics

# Interviewer Effects: Behavior

* Foundation of Standardized Interviewing (SI) is that if *Iwers* are interchangeable, response variance due to *Iwers* should be minimized
* But lots of evidence that SI not perfectly implemented by all *Iwers*
* Differences in behavior between one *Iwer* and the next likely to inflate *Iwer*-related variance ($\rho_{\text{int}}$)
  * Different distribution of answers collected by different *Iwers*
  * Reduces precision (increases confidence interval) of survey estimates
  * Measuring $\rho_{\text{int}}$ requires random assignment of *Rs* to *Iwers*; otherwise, diff’s. could reflect truth
  * If $\rho_{\text{int}}$ high, <u>implies</u> some responses less accurate than others — although do not know which responses are less accurate
* SI conducted to reduce diff.’s in *Iwer* behavior and so reduce $\rho_{\text{int}}$

# Estimating *Iwer* Variance

* Kish (1962) introduced $\rho_{\text{int}}$ (or $\text{rho}_{\text{int}}$) to capture the correlation between interviewers and the answers they elicit to a *particular Q* or *Iwer clustering*
  * sometimes called *intraclass correlation*

$$
\rho_{\text{int}} = \frac{\text{between}-\text{interviewer variance}}{\text{between}-\text{interviewer variance} + \text{within}-\text{interviewer variance}}
$$

* Between-interviewer variance is ideally zero
  * But never is!
  * Assumes random assignment of *I'wers* to *Rs*, , i.e., *interpenetrated* design
* Within-interviewer variance is (essentially) range of values for the variable (question) of interest; measures item’s ability to discriminate among *Rs*

17

<!-- page 18 -->

# Kish's formulation of $\rho_{\text{int}}$

$$
\rho_{int} = \frac{\left(\frac{V_a - V_b}{m}\right)}{\left(\frac{V_a - V_b}{m}\right) + V_b}
$$

* $V_a =$ between subjects mean square error in a one-way ANOVA with interviewers as the subjects
* $V_b =$ within subjects mean square error in ANOVA
* $m =$ total number of interviews conducted by the interviewer

# Interviewer Behavior and Interviewer Effects

* Particular *Iwer*-behaviors may affect $\rho_{\text{int}}$
* Mangione, Fowler & Louis (1992) coded *Iwer*-behaviors in audio-recorded interviews
* Computed correlation between each behavior and $\rho_{\text{int}}$
* Most correlations very small but those involving <u>probes</u> are larger and significant
* Probes are unscripted despite being central to standardized interviews

# Interviewer Effects for Different Types of Questions

* O’ Muircheartaigh & Campanelli (1999) observed significant $\rho_{\text{int}}$ in:
  * Attitude questions (26%) with Likert scales (33%)
  * Factual questions (26%)
* *Iwer* effects inflate variance about as much as geographical clustering
* Schnell & Kreuter (2005) examined 108 questions about crime; *Iwer* effects greater for
  * Sensitive than nonsensitive Qs
  * Nonfactual than factual Qs
  * Open than closed Qs
  * Difficult than easy Qs

# Impact of *Iwer* Effects on Precision of Estimates

* How much does $\rho_{\text{int}}$, i.e., *Iwer* clustering, inflate overall variance?
* Can calculate inflation by treating all other variance as a constant and adding $\rho_{\text{int}}$ multipied by the mean number of interviews for each *Iwer*
$$\text{deff}_{\text{int}} = 1 + \rho_{\text{int}} (m-1)$$
* Design Effect due to *Iwers* :
  * inflation of overall variance due to $\rho_{\text{int}}$
  * $m =$ average interviewer workload (# interviews)
  * $m$ moderates (i.e., amplifies or diminishes) impact of $\rho_{\text{int}}$
* Sometimes researchers use the square root of the Design Effect, called the Design Factor: ($\text{deft}$) is $\sqrt{deff}$
  * more useful for adjusting standard errors

21

<!-- page 22 -->

# Effect of *Iwer* Workload on Precision of Estimates

$$Deft_{int} = \sqrt{1 + \rho_{int}(m - 1)}$$

With a $\rho$ of .02 and mean # interviews ($m$) of 10,

$$\begin{aligned}
Deft_{int} &= \sqrt{1 + 0.02(10 - 1)} \\
&= \sqrt{1.18} \\
&= 1.087
\end{aligned}$$

If the average workload ($m$) were increased to 50,

$$\begin{aligned}
Deft &= \sqrt{1.98} \\
&= 1.41
\end{aligned}$$

# Impact of *Iwer* Effects on Precision

* $\rho_{\text{int}}$ permits computation of *design effects* due to *Iwers*
* Design Effect (deff) makes it possible to see effect of *Iwer* variance on overall variance
* Effective Sample Size quantifies impact of deff on power and precision:
$$\frac{n}{\textit{deff}}$$
    * e.g., if $n = 1000$ and $\textit{deff} = 1.26$, effective sample size $= 794$

# Response Validity versus *Iwer* Variance

* *Iwer* variance (i.e., $\rho_{\text{int}}$) is correlation between *Iwers* and responses they elicit
  * Although Standardized *Iwers* should be interchangeable, may be association between *Iwer* and responses they eliicit
  * If so, *suggests* *Iwer* is affecting answers; *implies* variation in data quality
* But we can't know for sure because $\rho_{\text{int}}$ is an indirect measure of data quality
  * Possible for $\rho_{\text{int}}$ to be low (good) and response validity to be low (bad) if <u>all interviewers</u> consistently collect the <u>wrong answer</u>
    * e.g., *Rs* asked if have recently visited doctor; survey intended to measure MD visits, but *Iwers* not instructed to or not permitted to define "doctor"; many *Rs* *may* include visits to non-MDs, e.g., PAs, podiatrists, chiropractors
    * $\rho_{\text{int}}$ would be low (uniform response distributions across interviewers) but accuracy would also be low (more "yes" answers than actual MD visits)
* Mean Square Error (MSE) is composite of *Iwer* variance ($\rho_{\text{int}}$) and (in)accuracy (squared bias)

# Does Conversational Interviewing increase *Iwer* variance?

* Proponents of SI argue that departure from scripted interaction will increase interviewer variance ($\rho_{\text{int}}$)
* If so, does the increase in interviewer variance undermine the advantage of CI for data quality
* West, et al. (2018) tested by calculating $\rho_{\text{int}}$ (IIC) and data quality
  * Data quality measured by (1) Comparing answers to administrative records (accuracy/bias) and (2) Response distributions in conjunction with definitions (inclusive* vs. exclusive** defs)
  * calculated $\rho_{\text{int}}$ (IIC) for all Qs for CI and SI
  * Where possible calculated MSE

\*e.g.: household member *includes* child away at college  
\*\*e.g: moving expenses *do not include* DIY expenses such as fuel, food, and lodging

# Does CI increase *Iwer* variance? (2)

- For 36 of 55 Qs (65.5%), interviewing technique did not significantly affect IICs or response distributions
- IICs significantly higher for one *Q* for CI than SI, marginally higher in 4
- Administrative data, required for MSE, available for 1 of the 5 Qs:
  - *longest uninterrupted period of employment in previous 20 years*
- Based on admin data, <u>CI led to significantly higher accuracy</u> than SI
  - <u>leading to smaller *MSE*\* (better quality)</u> for CI than SI despite increased IIC
  - CI produces wider confidence interval around more accurate point estimate than SI
- For 10 Qs in which estimates (e.g., means, %s) between techniques differed significantly, the results were more accurate (consistent with definitions) for CI than SI

\*MSE is squared bias (inaccuracy) plus variance (including IIC)

# Does CI increase *Iwer* variance? (3)

* Not much, if at all
  * For most *Q*s, IIC does not differ significantly between SI and CI
  * On the rare occasions when there are significant differences in IIC, CI improved accuracy more, offsetting higher IICs, resulting in smaller MSEs
* CI improves quality of reporting relative to SI consistent with previous findings *without notably increasing interviewer effects*
* *Note*: Duration of CIs ~1 min more than SI (ns)

# Summary: *Iwer* Effects Attributed to *Iwer*-Behavior

* Probing is key behavior related to $\rho_{\text{int}}$ – the only unscripted behavior in SI
  * But no greater in CI (where more unscripted *Iwer*-behavior) than SI, despite increased response accuracy in CI
* Impact of $\rho_{\text{int}}$ on precision is “diluted” by reduced average workload (fewer interviews per *Iwer*)
* $\rho_{\text{int}}$ generally not a direct measure of response accuracy
  * measures variance (correlation between *Iwers* and answers); if *R*s randomly assigned to *Iwers*, *Iwer-related* error likely reflects measurement error
* Response accuracy, when available, is a more definitive measure

# *Iwer* Effects: Enduring Characteristics

* Certain interviewer characteristics, primarily race and gender (but also age, class, ethnicity) have been shown to affect responses
  * systematically shifting answers in one direction – bias
* But only for Qs for which the attribute is relevant -- not for other Qs
  * e.g., race of interviewer only affects answers to Qs that concern race such as racial attitudes
* Distinct from literature on *Iwer* variance

# Gender of Interviewer

* Kane & Macaulay (1993) report that both male and female *Rs* gave more egalitarian answers to female than male *Iwers*… but for different items
  * More men reported sharing childcare when *Iwer* was female
  * More women supported feminist policy when *Iwer* was female
* Huddy et al. (1997) report numerous gender of interviewer effects in two pre-election surveys
  * Male and female *Rs* show gender of *Iwer* effects for same questions

# ROI Effects in Telephone Interviews

* Davis (1997) reports higher correlation between support for Jesse Jackson and racial consciousness for Black than White *Iwers*
* Davis and Silver (2003)
  * Black *Rs* answered fewer (3.4 vs 2.8) knowledge questions correctly – stereotype threat – when <u>perceived</u> *Iwer* to be Black
  * But no significant difference based on *Iwers'* <u>self-reported race</u>
  * For White *Rs* no effect of perceived or self-reported *Iwer* race
  * *Rs'* ability to judge race varies widely: estimates of “correct” race identification 14%-82%

# Interviewers: Summary/Conclusions

- Largest cost component of surveys involving interviewers
- Potentially large impact on survey errors across tasks
  - Recruiting, Respondent Selection
  - Data Collection
- *Iwer* effects: behavior, especially probing; fixed attributes e.g., race. gender
- All Interviewing Techniques have Pros and Cons:
  - SI weak at resolving misconceptions; CI can improve response accuracy but leads to longer interviews; little difference in interviewer variance
  - Personal style can motivate *Rs* but can lead to ingratiation; increased rapport seems generally to improve data quality
  - Commitment seems to improve data quality (with new evidence in web surveys)

# Interviewers: Summary/Conclusions (2)

- Despite growth of self-administered data collection, *Iwers* will remain vital component of survey data collection
- But probably in more targeted ways
  - e.g., difficult response tasks, exit polls, non-response follow-up to mailed survey invitations, need for physical/bio measures, samples of hard-to-reach populations, etc.
