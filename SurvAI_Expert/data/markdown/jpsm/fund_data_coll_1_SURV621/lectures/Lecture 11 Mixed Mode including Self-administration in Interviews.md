# Mixed-Mode Survey Design

* Researchers commonly combine survey modes in order to
  – Increase RRs, increase coverage, improve data quality
  – Optimize **trade-off** between different costs and errors
* Concern is creation of *mode effects*
  – different patterns of data between modes that are hard to attribute to anything other than mode
* Beyond collecting data, different modes can be used to contact/invite and communicate with *R*s
  – e.g., survey invitation in one mode, administration of questionnaire in another

# Principle of Hypothesis Testing

* Example Hypothesis:
  - For all potential survey respondents, if multiple survey modes are provided, then they will be more likely to participate in the survey.
  - The empirical test operationalizes each component into a concrete realization:
    * All potential survey respondents -> Specific sample persons
    * Survey mode -> Some set of survey modes
    * Propensity to participate -> Actual participation decisions in specific surveys
    * Hypothesized causal link -> Observed covariation in sample

# Hypothesis Testing and Validity to Causal Inference

* What are the potential inferential problems?
  1. Is there a large and reliable relationship between the cause and effect?
  2. Is the relationship causal?
     Covariation: When the values on one variable change, so do values on the other variables change, they are “co-related”
     Temporal Order: Changes on the independent variable precede changes on the dependent variable in time. X is the cause of Y and not the reverse.
     Elimination of rival hypotheses: Other potential causes are accounted for and ruled out. (i.e. plausible alternative hypotheses are eliminated)
  3. Does it involve the theoretical variables of interest?
  4. How generalizable is the relationship over samples, settings, methods, etc?

# Mixed-Mode Design: *Pre-notification*

- Different modes in *pre-notification* than *data collection*
  - Pre-notification alerts sample member to an upcoming survey invitation – not actually an invitation
- Examples:
  - Postal mail pre-notification for Web survey
- Goals:
  - Increase response rate by increasing study’s salience

# Mixed-Mode Design: *Pre-notification* and *Invitation*

* Bosnjak et al. (2008) tested impact on Web survey RR using three pre-notification (SMS, email, none) and two invitation (SMS, email) modes
* Nearly all participants owned a mobile phone
  - SMS most effective pre-notification mode
  - Email more effective invitation mode
  - SMS pre-notification + email invitation most successful mode combination for Web survey

# Mixed-Mode Design: *Data Collection*

* *Concurrent mixed-mode design*: One sample, cross-sectional, one q'aire but different sample members provide data in different modes
  * Generally used to improve coverage, e.g., to the extent web *Rs* are younger than in-person (FTF) *Rs*, mixing these modes may improve age representation; may also improve RR

* *Sequential mixed mode*: One sample, cross-sectional, sample members recruited in increasingly effective – and expensive – modes if previous contact attempts not successful
  * Generally used to increase response rates, e.g., mail, phone, in-person

* See de Leeuw, 2005, 2018 for more detail

24

<!-- page 25 -->

# Mixed-Mode Design: Data Collection (2)

* <u>*Switch modes within questionnaire*</u>: One sample; different modes for different parts of questionnaire for same *R*s
  - e.g., FTF-ACASI
* <u>*Longitudinal mixed mode*</u>: One sample, multiple time points; same persons measured with different modes at different times
  - e.g., interview modes may help engage sample members in study; once *R*s are committed to the study, a self-administered mode might be adequate
* <u>*Parallel (separate) mixed mode*</u>: Different samples, different modes
  - Typical in international surveys where some modes are more practical and common in some countries than others

❖ See de Leeuw, 2005 for more detail

# Concurrent Mixed-Mode

* Different modes for different subgroups of sample
  - e.g., Web for those with Internet access and device, mail for sample members without Internet access or device
  - Benefit: Increase coverage and response rate
  - Downside: Mode effects on measurement confounded with subgroups

26

# Sequential Mixed-Mode Design (Nonresponse Follow-up )

* Goal: Reduce nonresponse
  * Use one mode as reminder for another mode
  * e.g., contact mail *nonrespondents* by phone to remind them to fill out paper questionnaire

* Goal: Reduce cost of nonresponse follow-up
  * Use increasingly more expensive modes for increasingly fewer, increasingly reluctant respondents
  * e.g., initially mail, then telephone, then face-to-face
  * Risk of mode effects on measurement: later modes used for increasingly reluctant sample members who might be less conscientious if participate

# Mixed-Mode Follow-up

* Main findings from Dillman et al. (2009)
  - Offering modes sequentially can improve RR
  - But does little to resolve demographic bias due to nonresponse: in general follow-up does not change demographic composition of original sample
  - Mode effect: *Rs* in voice modes (live phone interview, IVR) gave more positive responses on bipolar scales than in visual modes (mail, Web)

# Switching Modes During Data Collection Session

* One sample, but different modes for different parts of questionnaire for same *R*s
  - Use different modes to collect different types of data (e.g., sensitive versus not sensitive) from the same *R*s
  - Improve data quality by increasing privacy of measurement
* Example: FTF + ACASI

# Longitudinal Mixed Mode

* One sample, same persons/households in panel survey, measured with different modes at different times
  - Use one mode in first wave, another mode for later waves
  - Reduces costs, if start with more expensive mode
  - Confounds actual change over time with mode effects on measurement
  - e.g., PSID and CPS – use face-to-face for initial wave, mostly telephone thereafter
* Alternative: Collect email address in early wave(s) and invite to subsequent online waves via email

31

# Parallel (Separate Samples) Mixed Mode

* Mainly used for comparative studies to accommodate different regional survey traditions or practical constraints (coverage, literacy, etc.)
  * Sometimes even different times and questionnaires
  * Mode effects may cause differences by country, state, or region, although many differences beside mode, including data collection organization
* Examples:
  * ISSP, ESS, BRFSS – different modes in different countries or states

# Mode Choice

* Designers sometimes offer *Rs* a choice of data collection mode
  - Particularly well-suited to Concurrent Mixed Mode designs (usually no choice -- different frames used for different modes)
* Evidence suggests mode choice likely to <u>reduce</u> RR (relative to no choice) but <u>improve</u> data quality for those who do participate

33

<!-- page 34 -->

# Mode Choice: *Response Rate*

* Mode choice seems to reduce participation
  - Medway and Fulton (2012) meta-analysis
  - Reviewed 19 studies that mailed invitations to sample members and included paper questionnaire; random $\frac{1}{2}$ *Rs* also offered web option
    * Choosing the web option requires typing the printed URL into device
  - Across the 19 studies, response rate was significantly lower overall in *choice* than *mail only* condition
    * RR lower in 17 out of 19 studies; significantly lower in 8

34

<!-- page 35 -->

# Mode Choice: *Response Rate* (2)

* Medway & Fulton propose that the negative impact of mode choice on response rate is due to:
  1. Paradox of Choice: simply being able to choose, makes both options less attractive than either alone
  2. Switching Costs: break in response process increases chances of losing URL or forgetting that intended to switch to web
  3. Usability/Connectivity problems (but the 3 studies that looked into this found little support for this with web completion > 80%)

35

<!-- page 36 -->

# Mode Choice: *Response Rate (3)*

* Push-to-web (Millar & Dillman, 2011): goal to maximize Web RR
  * Assumes there are benefits to Web over mail data collection
* In two experiments, postal mail invitation offering concurrent mode choice (Mail or Web) was expected to – but did not – improve RR beyond RR for Mail only
* But postal mail invitation offering concurrent choice (Mail or Web) followed up with email invitation that included link to web survey, led to RR comparable to Mail only
* Approach requires sampling frame with both primary email and street address for all sample members, so applicable to relatively narrow set of populations
* Dillman (2017) argues that initially contacting sample members via mail increases legitimacy of survey and allows inclusion of cash incentive; following with email invitation will produce RRs comparable to mail alone but with benefits of web
* In 10 tests, never quite produced equivalent mail-web and mail RRs but certainly shifted some respondents to web

# Mode Choice: *Data Quality*

* Higher quality data when *Rs* choose mode (irrespective of mode)
* Conrad et al. (2017)
  - invited sample members in 1 of 4 modes :
    Human Voice, Automated Voice, Human Text, Automated Text
  - Mode Choice Group: ½ *Rs* were required to choose interview mode – could be same as contact mode or one of the other three
  - Assigned Mode Group: Other ½ *Rs* assigned to participate in 1 of the 4 modes

# Mode Choice by Respondent: *Data Quality* (2)

* Mode Choice, compared to Assigned Mode, led to:
    1. Less rounding
    2. Less straightlining
    3. Fewer breakoffs
    4. Greater satisfaction with interview
* *R*s could have chosen mode that requires least effort
* But *R*s’ choices do <u>not</u> seem driven by effort reduction: most common choice is Automated Text, enabling them to respond when have time to answer thoughtfully

<!-- page 41 -->

# Mode Choice: *Response Rate* (redux)

* Mode Choice led to slightly, but significantly, lower RR (52.1%) than Assigned Mode (55.9%), as in Medway & Fulton, 2012
* Not due to Paradox of Choice (Conrad et al. 2017):
  * RR no lower for *Rs* who chose to stay (participate) in invitation mode than for those assigned to a mode, i.e., choosing to stay in contact mode is a choice
  * but significantly lower for Mode Choice *Rs* who switched modes
* Suggests effect of choice on RR is largely due to Switching Costs

# Mode Choice vs. Mode Preference

* Choosing a mode seems likely to motivate at least some *Rs* to participate and to do so conscientiously
  - But does seem to lower RR
* Inviting *Rs* to participate in a survey conducted in a mode they have previously indicated they prefer to other modes, may help improve data quality w/o reducing participation
* But may not be practical in most cross-sectional surveys because *Rs'* preferences are not known

# Mode Preference: *Response Rate*

* Response rates higher if interviewed in preferred mode
  - Preferred mode $\neq$ chosen mode: *Rs* may happen to participate in preferred mode but usually due to random assignment
  - Olson et al. (2012) asked *Rs* their preferred mode in telephone survey:

    *If you received a request to do another survey like this one, would you prefer to participate in an in-person interview at your home, an interview on your home phone, an interview on your cell phone, a paper survey sent by mail, or a survey on the Internet?*

  - One year later, same *Rs* invited to participate in another survey; randomly assigned to mail or web modes (or a sequence of mail-web or web-mail):
    * Some *Rs* answered in preferred mode, and some did not

# Summary/Discussion: Mixed Mode Designs

* Mixing modes to promote response rate and reduce coverage error is commonly used
  - Mode of Prenotification and Invitation vs. Data Collection
  - Different designs for mixing data collection modes, e.g., e.g., concurrent vs. longitudinal mixed modes
* Mode Choice is likely expected by sample members as number of communication modes proliferates
  - Although seems to reduce RRs, mode choice seems to increase data quality and satisfaction for those who complete interview
  - Reduction in RRs seems to be due to switching costs (changing modes)

# Switching Modes within Interview, from Interviewer- to Self-Administration

* Many demonstrations that allowing/requiring *Rs* to self-administer sensitive questions on the device used by *Iwer* for non-sensitive questions increases disclosure of sensitive info (i.e., socially undesirable answers) compared to *Iwer*
* Computer Assisted Self-Administered Interviewing (CASI) and Audio CASI (ACASI) routinely used to collect sensitive info in personal visits
* Tourangeau & Smith (1996) compared disclosure with and without interviewer
  * In general, men report 2-4 times as many heterosexual sex partners as women; presumed due to opposite directions of social desirability bias by gender
  * Self-administration reduces gender-based asymmetry for # reported sex partners; presumed due to reduced social desirability
  * Self-administration increases some reports of illicit drug use
  * ACASI produced more disclosure (compared to in-person) than CASI

# Does Audio Self-Administration Increase Disclosure?

* Immersive audio (e.g., headphones) seems to disconnect listeners from surroundings
  - When both audio and visual input, people seem to give more attention to the audio
  - e.g., mobile phones conversations while driving (in simulator) led to 2-fold increase in failure to detect traffic signs and, when signals detected, slower RTs (Strayer & Johnston, 2001)

* But Couper, Tourangeau & Marvin (2009) find no differences between ACASI and CASI (text-only) in reporting sensitive items in survey of fertility (NSFG)
  - May be due to non-use of audio (15% in NSFG pretest, 36% in NSFG Main Study) or incomplete playing of audio (*Rs* answer in 80% of play time for sound files)
  - No difference due to presence of others
  - No literacy advantage, at least those with lowest levels of education do not report differently in ACASI than CASI (text-only)

# Impact of Third Parties During Self-Administered Questionnaires

* Does presence of household members, during self-administration reduce sense of privacy enough to reduce disclosure?
* Reports of drug and alcohol use by adolescents via CASI and paper SAQ affected by presence of parents (Acquilino et al. 2000)
  – Less reported marijuana and alcohol use when parents or siblings present
  – Effects of parents on marijuana use observed for paper SAQ but not CASI
  – For older *Rs*, cohabiting partners have no effect, but presence of children reduce disclosure

* Tourangeau & Smith (1998) report greater advantage for CASI than SAQ (for some measures)

# Computerization and Self-administration (3)

* Meta-analysis (Gnambs & Kaspar, 2015) of 39 published articles with at least one comparison of disclosure in computerized (CASI/ACASI) vs. paper (SAQ) self-administration
  - 460 effect sizes compared
  - Predicted more disclosure in computerized self-administration than in paper SAQ because of greater anonymity, immersion, impartiality
* Odds ratio of disclosure in Computer to Paper: $\text{OR}=\text{pC}/\text{pP}$
* Overall, significantly more disclosure for computer than paper
* Effect increased as sensitivity increased:
  - measure of sensitivity available for 283 out of 343 effect sizes

# Interactive Voice Response (IVR)

* Audio-recorded spoken *Qs* presented via phone
* Typically, *Rs* answer via touchtone data entry (TDE)
* but in some implementations, *Rs* speak their answers (Speech IVR)
* When used to collect sensitive information, called T-ACASI

# IVR (cont’d)

1. Outbound\* IVR
    a. *Iwer* phones *R* and switches to automated system for sensitive *Q*s
        * In addition to “T-ACASI” also referred to as “Recruit and Switch”

# Statistical Error Model (Lee et al., 2016)

* Let's consider a particular observation under the influence of these errors under a <u>stochastic mechanism</u>. Let $U$ be a population of size $N$, and the study goal be estimation of a mean $\mu$ for a population characteristic. For a unit $j$ in $U$, the measurement process generates an observed value operationalized through $y_j$ as:

$$y_{j,MEA} = m_j \mu_j$$

* Here measurement error, $m_j$, is a function of the measured variable itself ($y_j$) and a vector of parameters, say $\Psi_{MEA}$, in $m_j = f(y_j, \Psi_{MEA})$. $\Psi_{MEA}$ may include observed auxiliary variables, and the function $f(y_j, \Psi_{MEA})$ can take any form (for example, linear or logistic regression model, generalized additive model).

# Statistical Error Model

* For sample-based data collection, a sample $S$ is drawn from a frame $C$, and sample units respond at less than a 100% rate. In the total error framework, frame coverage, sampling error, and nonresponse error play a role in observing the value of $y_j$ in a series of conditional models:

$$
y_{j,COV} = c_j y_{j,MEA}, \text{ where } j \in U,
$$

$$
y_{j,SAM} = s_j y_{j,COV}, \text{ where } j \in C, \text{ and}
$$

$$
y_{j,RES} = r_j y_{j,SAM}, \text{ where } j \in S.
$$

$c_j, s_j,$ and $r_j$ are indicators of being covered in the frame, selected in the sample, and responding to the survey, respectively, functions of $y_j$ and some parameters relevant to each of these sources of error.

# Statistical Error Model

* For instance, the sample selection error is

$$s_j = f(y_j, \Psi_{SAM}) = \begin{cases} 1, & if \ j \in S \\ 0, & otherwise. \end{cases}$$

In survey estimation the inverse of these probabilities may be used as adjustment weights for $y_{j,RES}$, the set of sample responses.

# Existing Mode Effect Evaluation Methods

* Current methods for evaluating mode effects focus on unconfounding the nonrandom selection of modes and mode effects in mixed-mode surveys.
* Regression model methods control for the nonrandom selection analytically (Jäckle et al., 2010).
* Mixture distribution method, introduced by Vannieuwenhuyze et al. (2010, 2012), computes the selection and mode effects for distribution parameter estimates, such as mean of a variable of interest, by defining as having a mixture distribution given the mixed-mode survey design.

# Existing Mode Effect Evaluation Methods

* Using a parallel single mode survey and assuming the same population and measurement properties, mathematically it is possible to show that parameters related to the selection and the mode effects can be quantified for a two-mode survey.
* To do these comparisons, the mixture mode distribution method relies on two key assumptions: completeness and representativity.
  - single-mode survey and the mixed-mode survey are unbiased estimates for the same survey population with respect to the non-observational survey error.
* In practice, this could be a strong assumption as the mixed-mode surveys are usually conducted to minimize the nonresponse bias under a certain budget constraint.
* On the other hand, the method conceptualizes the confounding nature of mode choice and mode effects.
* Additionally, the method enables the computation of required sample sizes to detect mode effects.
