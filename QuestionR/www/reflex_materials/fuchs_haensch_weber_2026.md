Abstract
Designing survey questions is easy; however designing good survey questions is a complex
task. Large language models (LLMs) have the potential to support this task by automating parts of the item-generation process, but their suitability for survey research has not
yet been systematically evaluated. Published research in this area remains sparse, and little is known about the quality and characteristics of survey items generated by LLMs or
the factors influencing their performance. This work provides the first in-depth analysis of
LLM-based survey item generation and systematically evaluates how different design choices
affect item quality. Five LLMs, namely GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-oss-20B, LLaMA 3.1
8B, and LLaMA 3.1 70B, were used to generate survey items on four substantive domains:
work, living conditions, national politics, and recent politics. We additionally evaluate three
prompting strategies: zero-shot, role, and chain-of-thought prompting. To assess the quality
of the generated survey items, we use the Survey Quality Predictor (SQP), a tool for estimating the quality of attitudinal survey items based on codings of their formal and linguistic

characteristics. To code these characteristics, we used an LLM-assisted procedure.
The findings show striking differences in survey item characteristics across the different models and prompting techniques. Both the choice of model and the prompting technique
employed influence the quality of LLM-generated survey items. Closed-source GPT models
generally produce more consistent items than open-source LLaMA models. Overall, chainof-thought prompting achieved the best results. GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and LLaMA 3.1

Introduction

Survey design is often treated as a straightforward task, yet it hinges on methodological rigor
rather than mere question wording. Producing items that are clear, reliable, and unbiased
remains a resource-intensive task requiring specialized expertise. AI-based tools and particularly large language models (LLMs) are attracting attention because they appear to compress
this expertise into seconds: generating alternative wordings, anticipating misunderstandings,
and systematically varying formulations. Whether this promise holds for survey research,
however, remains largely untested.
In this paper, we want to advance this field of survey methodology by providing the
first in-depth examination of LLM-based survey item generation. We broaden the scope
of existing research by providing a systematic analysis of key factors such as model size,
accessibility, and topical domain. Moreover, we analyze the characteristics of the generated
items and evaluate their quality. Figure 1 shows the workflow of the present study.

Generation of survey items. One line of research focuses on using LLMs to generate
survey items. Earlier research has shown promise in using LLMs to generate highly relevant and easy-to-understand survey questions, providing response options that accurately
reflect the question topic (Buskirk, Eck, and Timbrook 2025; Maiorino et al. 2023; Padgett,
Maiorino, and Gutierrez 2024). Researchers also apply LLMs in specialized contexts, such
as generating multiple-choice questions in the educational context (Al Faraby, Romadhony,

5

## Page 6

and Adiwijaya 2024; Doughty et al. 2024). Yet generating survey items with LLMs poses
structural challenges, such as overproducing open-ended questions or “yes/no” answer scales
and struggling with maintaining a logical question order (Padgett, Maiorino, and Gutierrez 2024). Progress in using LLMs to generate survey items remains limited, and only few
studies address this topic. One likely reason is that evaluating the quality of such generated
outputs is a complex tasks. Addressing this gap requires further research to reduce validity
and measurement error by ensuring that items accurately measure the intended construct
and are comprehensible for the respondents. Building on this research, this work looks at
a broader perspective. It examines how different LLMs generate survey questions, and how
factors such as model size, open- vs. closed-source models, and prompt design impact the
quality of the generated items.

Prompting techniques for generation of survey items. A recent experimental study
by Buskirk, Eck, and Timbrook 2025 examined how to design prompts for generating survey items on different topics (physical health, income, health insurance coverage, maternal
relationship, and substance/drug use) with ChatGPT in a zero-shot setting. They varied
context, task specification, and output format guidance, combining different survey topics,
prompt phrasing, and response format instructions. The study found that prompts including the word “survey” produced items that followed questionnaire design conventions more
closely, contained fewer unfamiliar terms, and were easier to read. Prompts that combined
“survey” with “response options” performed even better. These findings provide practical
guidance on how to design prompts for survey question generation.
Building on this work, we expand the analysis by Buskirk, Eck, and Timbrook 2025

Measuring Survey Quality with SQP

Measuring the quality of survey items can be done using a variety of techniques (Presser et
al. 2004), such as cognitive interviewing (Willis 2005), expert reviews (Forsyth, Rothgeb, and
Willis 2004), latent class analysis (LCA; Kreuter, Yan, and Tourangeau 2008), or quantitative
measures of reliability and validity (Saris and I. N. Gallhofer 2014).
This latter approach is the base for the Survey Quality Predictor (SQP), which differs
from other approaches by estimating the reliability, validity, and overall quality of survey
items using formal and linguistic codings of item characteristics. It then draws on an extensive database of previously evaluated items to predict measurement quality, enabling
researchers to anticipate and improve the performance of items prior to data collection.
To build SQP, researchers collected cumulative knowledge about the quality of survey
items based on previously conducted Multitrait-Multimethod (MTMM) experiments across
different topics, countries, and survey organizations, including extensive data from the European Social Survey2 (ESS). To analyze the data from these experiments, they applied
2. https://www.europeansocialsurvey.org.

7

## Page 8

the True Score Model (Saris and Andrews 1991), which distinguishes between validity and
reliability, the former referring to systematic and the latter to random measurement error.
They then coded the items’ characteristics (e.g., number of response categories or number of
words in the request) and linked these characteristics to the quality estimates obtained from
the MTMM experiments. To predict the quality of new survey items, the study adapted
a meta-analysis using random forests of regression trees (Breiman 2001), averaging predictions across multiple regression trees to generate predictions for new survey items (Felderer
et al. 2024; Saris and I. N. Gallhofer 2014).
The version used to assess the quality of the generated survey items in this work is
SQP 3.0, which is based on more than 600 MTMM experiments (Felderer et al. 2024). As
SQP only requires the question text and the coded item characteristics (see subsection 4.4
and Table A.1 in Appendix for a complete list of characteristics), it is a well-suited approach
for evaluating survey items generated by LLMs. Many of the characteristics that need to
be coded overlap with aspects emphasized in established guidelines for writing high-quality
survey questions, such as clarity and structure of the question, and specific response scale
characteristics (DeCastellarnau 2017; Groves et al. 2009; Krosnick and Presser 2009; Lenzner,
Kaczmirek, and Lenzner 2009; Morrison, Dillman, and Christian 2010; Schaeffer and Dykema
2020). The result of SQP 3.0 is an estimate of the three measurements: reliability, validity,
and their product, quality. The performance of the generated survey questions in this work
is assessed with these three measurements. Beyond prediction, SQP also provides an openaccess database3 with more than 75,000 coded survey items across 28 languages and 33
countries, including quality estimates (Repke, Neuert, and Weber 2023). This work uses the

4.2

Choice of Large Language Models

The survey items for the specified survey domains were generated using five large language
models (LLMs): the closed-source models GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI 2024), the
open-source models LLaMA 3.1 8B 4,5 and LLaMA 3.1 70B 4,6 (Grattafiori et al. 2024), and
the newly released open-source model GPT-oss-20B (OpenAI 2025). These models were
selected to cover a range of model sizes and architectures. The five selected models include
smaller models (GPT-4o-mini and LLaMA 3.1 8B ), medium-scale models (GPT-oss-20B ),
and larger models (GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.1 70B )7 .

All five models were configured with the same temperature, top-p, and maximum
token length hyperparameter settings. The temperature (a value between 0 and 1) controls
the randomness of the LLM’s output and was set to 0.8. A higher value leads to more
diverse responses, while lower temperature makes the output of the LLM more deterministic,
i.e., the most likely predictions are favored. Hence to generate varied yet coherent survey
item formulations, we chose a moderate temperature. The top-p parameter, which also
ranges from 0 to 1, defines how many of the most probable tokens the model considers when
generating text. It was set to 0.8, i.e., that only the most likely words are sampled until
their combined probability reaches 0.8. The maximum output token length was set to 150
to prevent the generation of overly long responses while still giving the model enough token
length to generate an introduction text, a request for an answer, and a response scale.

4.3

Prompting Techniques

For each of the selected LLMs and the different survey domains, three prompting techniques
were used to generate survey items: zero-shot prompting, role prompting, and chain-ofthought (CoT) prompting. These different techniques were chosen to explore how variations
in the structure of prompts given to the LLMs influence the quality and characteristics of
the generated survey items. The prompt formulations were developed based on previous
research on effective prompt design (Schulhoff et al. 2024; White et al. 2023) as well as
prior work on prompts in survey design, which emphasizes the inclusion of explicit surveyrelated instructions (e.g., “survey” and “response options”) in the prompt (Buskirk, Eck,
and Timbrook 2025).

12

## Page 13

In the zero-shot prompting approach, the model received a clear description of the task
without any examples of the desired output (Radford et al. 2019; Schulhoff et al. 2024).
With this prompting technique, the models’ performance can be tested purely on the given
instructions and only relying on its pre-trained knowledge. The second approach chosen to
generate the survey item is role prompting. Sometimes referred to as persona prompting, role
prompting assigns a specific role to the LLM in the prompt. Here, the model is instructed
to act as an expert survey methodologist, in addition to the clear task instructions without
examples, such as in the zero-shot prompt. The third technique, CoT prompting, encourages
the model to think in reasoning steps before giving its final output. The CoT prompt gives
the instruction to think step by step. First, the model is instructed to think about how to
investigate the concept of the survey item. Then, it is instructed to think about the survey
question, followed by the optional introduction text and the response scale.
Each prompt is provided with one of the four survey domains and one concept of
interest. The corresponding prompts can be found in A.2. This combination of the five
models, the four survey domains, with 60 concepts of interest each, and the three prompting
strategies resulted in 900 prompts for survey item generation (see Figure 3).

Comparison of Survey Item Characteristics

Characteristics of the request for an answer. Requests for an answer can be formulated in different ways. They can be phrased as indirect request, such as, “Would you say
that...?” or “Please indicate ...,” as direct request, e.g., “How satisfied are you...?”, or with
no explicit request, commonly used in question batteries that present a series of statements.
Across all models and prompting techniques, requests for an answer are mostly formulated
as direct requests (see Figure 5). However, for GPT-4o with zero-shot and role prompting,
LLaMA 3.1 8B with role prompting, and LLaMA 3.1 70B with zero-shot and role prompting,
indirect requests occurred nearly as often as direct requests.

5.2

Evaluation and Comparison of Survey Item Quality

Finally, we evaluate and discuss the validity (v 2 ), reliability (r2 ), and overall quality (q 2 ) of
the survey items generated by each model and prompting technique.
One approach to evaluating the quality of generated survey items relies on Cronbach’s
alpha cut-off points (see Pirralha and Weber 2020), expressing quality on a scale from 0 to
1, where 0 indicates no measurement of the construct of interest and 1 indicates perfect
measurement. However, perfect measurement is unattainable in practice, as survey items
are always subject to measurement error and hence can never reach the maximum of 1.
Instead of interpreting quality scores relative to this unattainable upper bound, the quality
of the LLM-generated survey items is compared with empirically observed quality values from
the SQP database. The SQP database contains 437 English survey items with authorized

24

## Page 25

codings, i.e., the items were coded under a controlled and supervised coding procedure. The
observed quality values of these survey items ranges from 0.289 (lowest) to 0.749 (highest),
reflecting the empirical bounds of survey item quality under real-world conditions. The same
approach is used to compare the validity and the reliability of the survey items.

Validity of generated survey items. Validity measures the strength of the relationship between the concept of interest and the true score, after accounting for the method
effect. High validity indicates that a survey item accurately captures the construct it is
intended to measure, independent of the method; whereas low validity implies that the observed responses are largely driven by systematic error and only weakly related to the target
construct. The observed validity from the English survey items in the SQP database ranges
from 0.924 to 0.623 (see Figure A.6 in Appendix). When comparing the models, the closedsource GPT and the open-source LLaMA models achieve similar mean and median validity
(≈ 0.77 − 0.79). However, GPT models show smaller variability compared to the LLaMA
models, especially in combination with CoT prompting (see Figure 11).
Compared to the validity of the survey items from the SQP database, the average
validity is only slightly higher for the SQP items (0.81). Table A.9 in Appendix provides a
detailed summary of the descriptive statistics presented in Figure 11 and of the SQP items.

6

Conclusion

With this paper, we provide the first in-depth analysis of using AI for survey item design,
offering a foundation for understanding how LLMs perform in this context. We examined
the survey item characteristics and the quality of LLM-generated survey items, comparing
them across different pre-trained models, three prompting techniques, and various survey
topics. We assessed whether LLMs can consistently generate high-quality survey items and
showed how model choice and prompting strategies affect their performance across different
attitudinal topics.

6.1

Summary of Results

Across models and prompting techniques, the generated survey items show striking differences in their characteristics. Introduction texts, question phrasing, and response scale
formats vary greatly, especially across prompting techniques. GPT models mostly generate
similar items, regardless of model size and prompting technique. They mainly generate fivecategory, symmetric scales with medium correspondence between verbal and numeric labels,
and never including a ‘don’t know’ option. The characteristics of the LLaMA models differ
more between the different model sizes and different prompts.

29

## Page 30

In terms of quality, both model families achieve similar average levels of validity, reliability, and overall quality, although LLaMA models show greater variability and occasionally
produce unusable outputs, particularly the smaller LLaMA 3.1 8B model. The smaller GPT
model, GPT-4o-mini, often performs as well as or better than the larger counterpart, indicating that increased model size does not automatically improve survey item quality. The
results show that the 437 survey items from the SQP database exhibit a wider quality range,
spanning approximately from 0.29 to 0.75. In contrast, the LLM-generated survey items
show a much narrower quality range (from 0.37 to 0.66). Despite this difference, the average
quality of LLM-generated items is comparable to that of the SQP items, with SQP items
achieving only a slightly higher mean quality overall. This indicates that, although SQP
items cover a broader range of quality, LLM-generated survey items are able to reach a level
of quality similar to that of survey items currently used in practice.
Prompting also plays a role in survey item quality and survey item characteristics.
CoT prompting leads to better quality across all GPT models. The LLaMA models display
larger performance differences across prompting techniques, with the smaller LLaMA 3.1
8B model showing the greatest sensitivity to prompt type. For the larger LLaMA 3.1 70B
model, role prompting results in the highest variation in quality.
As Buskirk, Eck, and Timbrook 2025 pointed out in their study on identifying prompting strategies for generating survey items, small changes in the wording of prompts can affect
the quality of the generated survey items. We expand upon these findings by demonstrating
that not only precise wording but also explicit reasoning steps as part of CoT prompting
can affect and potentially improve item quality.

30

## Page 31

Overall, we find that LLMs can generate survey questions of comparable quality to
those used in practice and can therefore serve as assistive tools in survey design. We conclude
by recommending that researchers who seek to use LLMs for this purpose consider smaller
GPT models or larger LLaMA models, and apply CoT prompting to further enhance question
quality.

6.2

Limitations and Outlook

The assessed quality of the survey items is based on predicted values from the SQP model,
which depends on the underlying prediction algorithm. This reliance may influence the
reported quality outcomes. An alternative approach could have involved expert evaluations
to assess the quality of the LLM-generated survey items, as previously implemented in prior
research (e.g., Buskirk, Eck, and Timbrook 2025). Both SQP-based evaluations and expert
reviews have their advantages and disadvantages, as SQP allows efficient assessment of many
survey items without requiring extensive survey expertise but depends on a prediction model,
while expert evaluations provide broader perspectives on quality and detailed feedback but
can be influenced by subjectivity, individual biases, and disciplinary backgrounds. In this
study, we relied on SQP as it is currently the only openly available tool that enables largescale assessment of survey item quality.
This work sets the stage for further investigation into how LLMs can assist in the
survey design process and aims to inspire future research in this emerging area. Future
studies could expand the analysis by including a multilingual context, allowing researchers
to study LLM-generated survey items in different languages and cultural settings. This
31

## Page 32

could also include integrating LLM-generated translation to examine how effectively models
generate and translate survey items consistently across languages.
Due to the limitations of SQP, this paper focused on generating survey items for
attitudinal questions. Future studies could extend this work by examining how LLMs handle
other types of survey questions. Beyond generating individual items, future research could
also investigate how LLMs create entire survey questionnaires for specific topics. As previous
research has shown, question order can influence response quality (Garbarski, Schaeffer, and
Dykema 2014). Therefore, exploring how LLMs generate complete questionnaires could offer
more insight into how AI can help in survey design.

Generate one attitudinal survey question for the topic {survey domain}, asking the

introduction text/extra information if needed for the respondent to comprehend the
question, and one response scale without elaborating. Do not repeat the information
you have been given, and give your answer directly and without introductory phrases or
additional comments. The survey question should be in English, and it is for a general
population survey.

Role Prompting
Act as an expert survey methodologist. Generate one attitudinal survey question
for the topic {survey domain}, asking the respondent’s opinion on: {concept of interest}.
Provide only one survey question, the introduction text/extra information if needed for
the respondent to comprehend the question, and one response scale without elaborating.
Do not repeat the information you have been given, and give your answer directly and
without introductory phrases or additional comments. The survey question should be
in English, and it is for a general population survey.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting
Let’s think step by step. First, think about how to investigate ‘{concept of interest}’
within the topic of {survey domain}. Then, think about one single suitable attitudinal
survey question for a general population survey. Then, think about whether the attitudinal survey question needs an introduction text/extra information for the respondent to
comprehend the question. Finally, think about one suitable response scale for the survey
question you designed. Provide only one survey question, the introduction text/extra
information if needed for the respondent to comprehend the question, and one response
scale without elaborating. Do not repeat the information you have been given, and give
your answer directly and without introductory phrases or additional comments. The
